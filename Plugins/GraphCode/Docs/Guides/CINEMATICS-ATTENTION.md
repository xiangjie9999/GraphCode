# Direct audience attention and focus

Plan what matters at each moment and how the audience can perceive it. Start with
[presentation intent](CINEMATICS-INTENT.md) and the project's
[artistic treatment](CINEMATICS-DIRECTION.md). Attention is shaped by staging, performance,
composition, movement, light, sound and time together; it is not a camera setting.

The camera-placement principles in ASC's
[Where Do You Put the Camera?](https://theasc.com/article/shot-craft-where-do-you-put-the-camera/)
connect viewpoint, blocking, geography and reactions. The exercises below turn those relationships
into planning and review records. They are practical recommendations, not eye-tracking measurements
or universal predictions of what every viewer will notice.

## Separate dramatic, visual and optical focus

| Focus | Directing question | Typical correction |
|---|---|---|
| Dramatic priority | What action, thought or relationship changes the beat? | Clarify the behavior, evidence or information order. |
| Visual attention | Where should we look, and what should we notice afterward? | Restage, reframe, separate shapes, simplify competition or change timing. |
| Optical focus | What distance is sharp, and when should that sharpness transfer? | Adjust target/distance, depth of field, transfer timing and tracking through the shot. |

Sound can announce an unseen source, redirect a look, emphasize an action or deliberately withhold
information. Plan its relation to all three rows. A sharp face can still be visually lost against
an active background; a clearly visible prop can still be dramatically irrelevant.

## Write an attention record for each beat

Keep this with the beat sheet and board annotations:

```text
Beat / shot / intended audience knowledge:
Primary: what must be noticed now, and what change must be read?
Secondary: what should be available after that, or support it without competing?
Withheld: what should not become clear yet, and why?
Evidence/cues: staging, gesture, gaze, shape/value/color, motion and sound.
Timing: cue → discovery → reading/response → next emphasis.
Transfer: where attention enters from the preceding image and leaves for the next.
Optical focus: target/depth/transfer if needed; what must remain legible around it.
Risks: overlap, occlusion, competing movement, exposure, sound masking or insufficient time.
Review: what observation would show the intended reading or expose a problem?
```

Primary does not mean the image may contain only one interesting thing. A shared frame can invite
comparison or let the audience discover secondary information. Decide whether the beat requires a
precise order, simultaneous awareness, or an intentionally open scan. Do not hide essential action
and later assume every viewer found it.

## Use cues that belong to the art direction

### Staging, silhouette and relationships

Separate the decisive hand, face, prop or mechanism from confusing overlap. Use position, scale,
depth and negative space to expose the relationship. Check important shapes at the intended display
size, through the entire action. A readable pose can disappear during a reach or camera passage.

Eyelines and body orientation can connect people, objects and off-screen space. Establish a gaze
target; time the look so its destination or consequence can be understood. For a reaction-led reveal,
the listener's behavior may be the primary subject while the speaker remains secondary. Do not
automatically cut to whoever is talking.

### Value, color and detail hierarchy

Group large values before refining detail. Separate the important action through suitable subject/
background relationships, edges and local contrast. Check against the chosen treatment: a dim
intimate scene and a bright graphic comedy need different solutions. Lighting should belong to
the world and dramatic progression, not become an arbitrary spotlight that follows every hand.

Use color relationships established by the project; contrast or a recurring accent can guide a
particular reveal. Do not assume one color universally means danger, calm or importance. Surface
detail, reflections, emissives and specular motion can compete with the subject even if their
materials are individually attractive. Place richness where it supports the composition.

### Motion, performance and camera response

Stage a gaze shift, pause, redirection or gesture at the beat where it matters. Coordinate relevant
background motion and effects so they do not bury it. A still subject against an active crowd can
be effective when that contrast is readable; reducing every background to inactivity can lose the
world's character. Test the specific relationship.

A pan can follow attention or reveal a new subject. A push can develop proximity while an action
settles. A held camera can allow a small decision or background arrival to register. Choose from
[camera movement](CINEMATICS-CAMERA-MOVEMENT.md) after naming the intended change; constant reframing
can prevent the audience from finding a stable relationship.

### Sound and time

An off-screen sound can motivate the character's look before the image reveals its source. Its
direction, prominence and delay affect what the audience anticipates. Do not let music mask a cue
that supplies necessary information. Preserve silence or a quieter sound space when a small action
needs attention; judge the actual mix rather than a cue list.

Give unfamiliar objects, geography, expressions and changing relationships time to read. There is
no universal reading duration: evaluate subject size, complexity, motion, prior knowledge and the
viewing context. A longer hold cannot repair a hidden gesture; a clearer gesture may still need a
brief response before the next emphasis.

## Transfer attention through the shot and the cut

Plan the entry emphasis, cue, discovery, response and exit emphasis. For a reveal, decide whether
the audience follows the character, knows first or remains behind. Establish and test the withholding
condition: a camera move must not expose the answer before the intended cue unless that is the plan.

At each cut, identify the outgoing attention area and the incoming subject/relationship. Position,
action direction, eyeline, shape and sound can help connect them. Keeping related attention near
the same screen region can be useful; a deliberate displacement can create contrast when the new
view has enough cues and time. Neither alignment nor displacement is a universal requirement.

For a pan, inspect the passage as carefully as the landing: does motion lead us to the right
subject without an earlier false destination? For an action cut, can the important contact or
direction change be read across it? For a dissolve, does the overlap create competing subjects
or double contours that obscure the intended association? Use
[editing/transitions](CINEMATICS-EDITING-AND-TRANSITIONS.md) for the construction.

## Choose optical focus after staging the attention

Decide what must be readable together before selecting depth of field. A shared relationship or
mechanism may benefit from greater depth; a selective plane may isolate the relevant thought or
detail. Judge lens, filmback, camera/subject distances and aperture together in the actual view.
Do not choose shallow depth merely as a quality marker.

A rack focus can transfer attention between established planes, reveal a relationship or change
which detail dominates. Confirm both targets are compositionally available and the cue motivates
the transfer. Give the landing time to read. Follow the
[rack-focus procedure](CINEMATICS-CAMERA-MOVEMENT.md#rack-focus), then inspect tracking through
performance and camera motion. Sharpness alone does not make a tiny background action readable.

## Diagnose a missed beat at its cause

| Observation | Possible cause to inspect | Useful comparison |
|---|---|---|
| The viewer misses the hand's decision | Overlap, weak gesture, competing motion or premature cut | Clearer staging/gesture with the same camera, then compare timing if needed. |
| The reveal feels unmotivated | Missing cue, wrong eyeline or cut before the character's attention changes | Preserve the cue and compare the order of gaze, sound and reveal. |
| A bright window dominates the reaction | Value/reflection hierarchy contradicts the subject | Compare plausible light/composition changes while preserving the scene's treatment. |
| The pan lands but the new object is not understood | Weak landing composition or insufficient reading time | Compare a clearer landing and a held view; do not only slow the whole move. |
| Shallow focus makes the scene confusing | Essential relationship spans depth or focus transfer is early | Compare greater readable depth or a better staged transfer with the same intent. |
| A dissolve feels cluttered | Both images compete during overlap | Inspect the overlap; compare new framing, timing or a direct cut. |
| The story reads only with spoken explanation over playback | Necessary evidence is absent or its order is unclear | Revise boards/action/cues and review again without author narration. |

These are hypotheses, not automatic diagnoses. State what the comparison will distinguish, inspect
it, and record the result in the [decision log](CINEMATICS-PREPRODUCTION.md#keep-decisions-and-tests-concrete).

## Review with limited diagnostics, then in context

Review the whole passage at delivery speed and size with the intended sound. Inspect decisive
frames and the neighboring cut only after observing the uninterrupted result. Use thumbnail views
to check large masses and silhouette; use grayscale as a limited value-group check, especially
when the treatment depends on color. A simple desaturation is not a complete perceptual or display
test, and can remove a deliberately useful color distinction.

A muted pass helps isolate visual staging; a sound-only pass helps locate masking or weak cue
relationships. Neither replaces combined review. Check the actual delivery aspect/crop and focus
through motion. Ask a fresh viewer where they looked and what they understood when available;
record their account rather than claim eye-tracking evidence.

For the courier, inspect whether attention passes from the route to the dropped parcel, then
to the decision, helping action and its consequence. For an environment, inspect orientation,
relative scale and destination. For a product, inspect operation, contact and result. Judge focus
against the presentation's purpose, then carry corrections into boards, timing and production.
