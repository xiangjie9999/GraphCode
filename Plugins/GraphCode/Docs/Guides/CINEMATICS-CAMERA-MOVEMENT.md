# Execute cinematic camera movement

Use movement to develop attention, proximity, space and rhythm. Choose the intended effect in
[artistic direction](CINEMATICS-DIRECTION.md), solve [staging and framing](CINEMATICS-SHOTS.md),
then use this chapter to execute the move. The examples and practice exercises are original
recommendations, not universal speed, lens or emotion formulas.
Use [the GraphCode authoring path](CINEMATICS-GRAPHCODE.md#author-camera-moves-and-editorial-transitions)
for supported transform/lens channels and native handoffs.

Use [technique selection](CINEMATICS-TECHNIQUE-SELECTION.md) to compare hold/move/cut for the
intended presentation. Plan cues, significant images and landing in the
[frame ledger](CINEMATICS-STORYBOARDS.md#keep-an-explicit-frame-ledger), then judge motion continuously.

## Choose what the camera changes

First distinguish geometry from equipment. A pan/tilt rotates; a dolly/truck/pedestal translates;
a zoom changes focal length; a focus pull changes the sharp plane. A shot can combine them, but
each added change must make the staging or attention work better.

| Technique | What changes | Choose it when | Common failure to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Locked / planted camera | Viewpoint and framing remain deliberately fixed. | Performance, composition, stillness or an event entering/leaving the frame should carry the change. | Adding a drift simply because the image seems quiet; a fixed view still needs developed action. |
| Pan | Camera turns horizontally from its position. | Follow lateral action, connect people/places, investigate or reveal something outside the frame. | An unmotivated scan, missed landing or revealing the answer before the intended cue. |
| Tilt | Camera turns upward/downward. | Follow vertical movement or connect a detail with a taller subject/space. | Describing a tall asset without a payoff; losing the important face/gesture during the sweep. |
| Pedestal / boom | Camera position rises/falls. | Change sightline or foreground relationship while controlling viewing angle. | Using tilt when the scene needs a new vantage point, or drifting through geometry. |
| Dolly in / out | Camera moves toward/away from the subject. | Change proximity and spatial relationships during a realization, confrontation or reveal. | A decorative push that neither changes attention nor develops the beat. |
| Truck / lateral track | Camera position travels sideways. | Reveal through parallax, uncover an occluded object or accompany lateral action. | Calling it a pan; foreground crossings become distracting or hide the reveal. |
| Tracking / follow | Camera accompanies a moving subject on a designed route. | The journey and changing space belong to one continuous experience. | Constant mechanical centering or a camera that arrives before the subject without a viewpoint reason. |
| Orbit / arc | Camera travels around the subject. | Reveal a relationship or background, change alignment, or develop a stylized moment. | Circling without new information; changing the action axis and confusing the next cut. |
| Crane / jib / aerial move | Camera travels through height and space. | Connect a human detail with larger geography, disclose scale or enter/leave a scene. | An arbitrary flying path, abrupt scale changes or a landing unrelated to the next beat. |
| Zoom | Focal length changes from the chosen camera position. | Reframe optically, suggest observation, or use an established expressive zoom style. | Treating it as a dolly: it does not supply the positional change/parallax of camera travel. |
| Dolly zoom | Camera travel and focal change counteract subject-size change. | A conspicuous spatial disturbance belongs to the scene's subjective/stylized language. | Accidental subject growth, failed focus or a familiar effect substituting for a readable turning point. |
| Rack focus | Sharpness transfers between depth planes. | Both subjects belong in one composition and the change in attention is the event. | An unreadable first target, a background too insignificant to discover, or focus arriving before the thought. |

Handheld, shoulder, stabilized and mounted describe operating behavior as well as support.
ASC's [movement-tool survey](https://theasc.com/article/shot-craft-camera-movement/) relates these
choices to the intended scene. In a virtual camera, decide what the operator follows, what they
can anticipate and how they settle. Choose behavior consistently enough for a departure to mean
something. Combine moves after their individual jobs are clear.

Ask whether to move, cut or let blocking change the frame:

- **Move** when the relationship between the starting and ending views, the route or the time
  spent discovering something matters.
- **Cut** when the new viewpoint/information should arrive immediately, time should be omitted,
  or another performance provides the strongest next beat.
- **Hold** when the action can develop meaningfully inside the current composition. A character
  entering, turning or leaving may make the new image without camera travel or a cut.

## Shape motion through the edit

Choose camera behavior the scene can sustain: a planted observer, an operator following a person,
a controlled dolly, a vehicle-mounted view, or an explicitly stylized move. Design its acceleration,
settling, corrections and response. Let a character lead the camera when that serves the beat;
let the camera anticipate when foreknowledge is intended. A locked camera is a valid finished choice.

Give each move a start, development and landing. Check the entire path, not only its endpoints:
subject placement, speed, collisions/occlusion, unwanted rotation and the final hold. Inspect
curves for overshoot, drift, angular wrapping and unintended easing. Do not apply the same ease
to every axis merely because it is available. Constant tracking may need constant speed, while
an emotional arrival may need a designed settle.

## Rehearse and finish a pan

Adobe's [pan reference](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/video/production/cinematography/camera-shots-and-angles/pan-shot.html)
defines the basic horizontal turn and describes following, revealing and whip behavior. Build a
pan around an attention change, with a purposeful beginning, passage and landing.

### Decide how the camera knows where to look

| Pan behavior | Appropriate intention | Timing decision |
|---|---|---|
| Follow | Stay with an action as it crosses the space. | Decide whether the subject leads the camera and how much room remains toward travel. Accompany the action instead of forcing a preset pace onto it. |
| Reveal | Move from known information to something newly significant. | Choose the trigger and exact moment the new subject becomes readable. A slow reveal and a sudden discovery create different expectations. |
| Anticipatory | Let the audience discover an obstacle/destination before the character. | Leave the character deliberately, establish the relevant information, then stage their arrival or reaction. Preserve their route so anticipation stays legible. |
| Search / survey | Make the act of looking through a space part of the experience. | Give the search a question and discoveries; decide what causes each hesitation or continuation. An even scan of equally weighted detail rarely supplies a dramatic progression. |
| Whip / swish | Transfer attention with a fast, conspicuous turn. | Establish why attention snaps, prepare a usable arrival and decide whether the blur hides a cut. See [whip transitions](CINEMATICS-EDITING-AND-TRANSITIONS.md#design-a-hidden-cut-or-whip-transition). |

### Construct the pan in order

1. **Stage the action and destinations.** Place the original subject, new target and landmarks
   in a coherent space. Choose a camera position from which a turn can connect them. If a wall
   blocks the target from that position, translate or restage; rotation alone cannot see around it.
2. **Compose both ends.** Make the starting image worth seeing and the ending image worth arriving
   at. Set lens/filmback, height and focus around those purposes. Check the route between them,
   including clutter, bright distractions, tangencies and partial reveals.
3. **Mark the cue.** Use a look, sound, entrance, physical action or deliberate authorial decision.
   Specify whether the camera reacts, follows or leads. A head turn can begin the cue without
   requiring the camera to duplicate the actor's motion frame for frame.
4. **Shape the passage.** Rehearse angle and duration with the performance. Give a follow pan the
   speed needed to preserve the chosen composition. Give a reveal enough time to notice the
   destination without an empty journey. Include acceleration/deceleration where the chosen
   operator behavior requires it; avoid an identical ease preset on every move.
5. **Design the landing and next edit.** Settle without an unintended correction, or carry the
   motion into the next shot deliberately. Allow the new information/reaction to register. Keep
   working handles around the intended edit so its timing can change without rebuilding the move.
6. **Review at speed, then diagnose.** Watch through the actual camera at delivery rate with sound.
   Check when the destination first becomes visible, when viewers recognize it, and where their
   attention goes next. Then inspect framing, curves, focus and the neighboring cut frame by frame.

For a stationary pivot, a simple pan is primarily a rotation change; its position and focal
length can remain fixed. A constant-speed person crossing nearby does not necessarily require
constant angular camera speed. Judge the subject's movement in the frame rather than assume
equal world-space increments produce the desired image. Preserve intentional gaze/travel room;
keeping a face exactly centered at every instant can erase useful anticipation.

**Original timing exercise:** a reveal turns through 60 degrees in a six-second shot. Reserve
one second to establish the starting view, four seconds for the turn, and one second to read the
landing. The moving interval averages 60 / 4 = 15 degrees/second. Acceleration and settling alter
instantaneous speed; the average is a calculation, not a recommended pan speed. Test a shorter
route, another duration and a straight cut, then select the version that makes the discovery work.

Inspect the final output for judder, motion blur/smearing and lost detail. Confirm playback is
delivering frames correctly before diagnosing the camera path. Compare move speed, lens/framing,
frame rate and shutter/blur settings through the production's render path; change the variable
that addresses the observed defect. More render samples alone do not establish readable motion.
Do not change a project's delivery rate casually to repair one pan.

**Pan review:** can a viewer explain why we turned, what we discovered and how it connects to
the previous view? A smooth path with no useful landing still needs editorial/artistic revision.

## Execute travel, lens and operator choices

### Tilt or change camera height

Use a tilt to connect vertically separated information from the chosen position: a hand holding
a rope → the person suspended above, or a gaze downward → what lies below. Compose both views,
mark the cue, control when the destination is recognizable and give it an appropriate landing.
Inspect intermediate faces, silhouettes and background tangencies. Rehearse focus if the
important target's distance changes. A tilt changes where we look from the existing vantage point.

Use a pedestal/rise when the vantage point itself must change: see over a barrier, change a
foreground overlap, or rise with someone standing. Stage the required new height and maintain
or deliberately change the viewing angle. Check the path's clearances and reveal timing. A
straight pedestal and a crane's arc have different geometry even if both raise the camera.
Compare tilt, rise and a cut when height alone is not the dramatic point.

### Push in and pull back

Choose the emotional or informational change first. For a realization, try starting the push
when the character begins to understand and arriving when their decision becomes visible.
Compare starting earlier: that gives the camera more foreknowledge. Neither timing is automatic.
A pullback can reveal another person listening or make the environment newly significant.

Stage the final frame before the path. Set the travel distance and lens together; keep useful
facial scale, background relationship and foreground crossings throughout. Rehearse focus as
distance changes. A tiny push that never alters the experience may be unnecessary; a large push
can overwhelm a restrained performance. Test a locked version as a meaningful comparison.

### Track beside, follow behind or lead in front

Choose the relationship to the traveler. Following behind can share discovery; leading can
keep the face accessible while showing where they came from; a side track can compare them with
people or spaces they pass. Establish the route and the key encounters before refining speed.

Set the camera-to-subject relationship at important moments, then connect those moments. Let
obstructions reveal or separate intentionally. A camera may stop while the subject continues,
changing the relationship; it need not accompany them indefinitely. Check corners, acceleration,
body scale, background speed, walking rhythm and the final geography in the cut. Avoid letting
a target constraint erase the planned lead/lag, jump between targets or create a sudden spin.

### Orbit, rise and descend

For an orbit, identify the new background/relationship that the arc reveals. Rehearse the angle
where that information appears, the performer's eyeline and the next shot's axis. A full circle
is optional; stop where the dramatic job is complete. Check foreground occlusion and focus
through every part of the arc, not only a ring of attractive stills.

For a crane-like move, connect specific scales: a hand at a gate → the platform → the departing
tram and city. Compose the human moment and final geography; the rise between them must keep
attention useful. A descent can reverse that progression. Establish acceleration, height,
clearances and the landing; passing through solid architecture needs a designed stylized/VFX
transition. Virtual freedom still needs an intelligible viewing experience.

### Zoom and dolly zoom

Use a zoom if optical reframing belongs to the visual language. Choose whether it calls attention
to itself or quietly changes the view. Recheck focus, aperture/depth response and composition
during the focal change. Distinguish it from moving closer before selecting the technique.

For a dolly zoom, choose the subject anchor, starting and ending camera distances and target
screen size. Counter the travel with focal length, refine the framing, then solve focus across
the move. A background must have useful depth for its spatial change to read. Judge the actual
images rather than rely on a distance/focal-length ratio as final proof. Use this conspicuous
distortion of spatial experience only when its place in the film is clear.

### Rack focus

Stage two meaningful depth planes in a composition that supports both. Establish the first
focus target, introduce the cue for shifting attention and give the second target a readable
arrival. Set distances from the actual staging and choose sufficient separation/aperture for the
transfer to read. Check the focus ramp against the thought, not merely the actor's position.

If viewers cannot identify what becomes important, revise its placement, contrast or timing.
If both targets must remain legible together, deeper focus or another frame may serve better.
Check tracking-focus behavior against the intended manual/subjective choice; an automatically
sharp moving face is not the same as an authored transfer of attention.

### Handheld and stabilized behavior

Design an operator's response: how they track, reframe, absorb a stop and find a hold. Add motion
at causes such as steps, impacts or a hurried correction, with amplitude suited to the lens and
viewing size. Inspect readable faces and contacts during that motion. Avoid unrelated noise on
every axis or a shake layer that conceals weak animation.

A stabilized move can be responsive and purposeful without constant floating. A locked view
can be intense. Choose continuity of operating style across the scene, then make a departure
count. Apply the project's viewer-comfort requirements to sustained or aggressive camera motion.

## Review and practice

Record the cue, starting composition, passage, landing and intended audience change in
[the shot record](CINEMATICS.md#keep-a-useful-shot-record). Play with the performance and sound,
then in the neighboring edit. Inspect attention, frame edges, speed changes, focus, occlusion
and angular wrapping. Verify the exported sequence rather than approve only endpoints.

- **Pan versus cut:** stage a look toward a sound source. Make a reactive pan, an anticipatory
  pan and a cut to the source. Compare who knows what first and whether geography is clear.
- **Travel versus zoom:** reframe one subject with a dolly and a zoom. Compare parallax,
  background relationship and emotional distance while preserving a useful final frame.
- **Follow behavior:** let a traveler lead, accompany them, then hold as they leave. Compare
  how each camera relationship changes the sense of agency and discovery.
