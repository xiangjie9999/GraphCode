# Direct the artistic experience of a cinematic

Decide what the scene means and how the audience should experience it. This chapter covers the
emotional progression, subtext, composition, motifs, color/light and sound perspective.
Use [the workflow](CINEMATICS.md) to organize production, [shot design](CINEMATICS-SHOTS.md) to
stage and frame it, and [GraphCode](CINEMATICS-GRAPHCODE.md) to realize the approved choices.

## Write the scene's purpose

Write the canonical brief with [CINEMATICS-INTENT.md](CINEMATICS-INTENT.md): audience, purpose,
viewpoint, information progression, delivery, scope and success criteria. Then develop the
treatment here. Save both in the [director's packet](CINEMATICS-PREPRODUCTION.md); make necessary
choices before production and use planned rough studies to resolve uncertain views.

## Develop the artistic treatment

Write a short treatment describing how the scene should feel and how its artistic choices change
as it develops. Use specific images, sounds and performances as references. Explain what each
reference teaches—distance between people, the weight of a silence, the texture of a room, the
moment a composition opens up—rather than collecting a mood board of unrelated attractive frames.

Disney's [visual-development practice](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/visual-development/)
connects character/world design, color and composition to story themes and emotional moments.
Make a few key images or paintovers for the opening, turning point and ending, plus a rough
value/color strip showing their relationship. These are artistic hypotheses to test in the edit,
not substitutes for motion or performance. The following exercises are recommended ways to turn
a treatment into concrete choices; they are not universal rules about audience emotion.

### Shape an emotional progression

Describe a progression the audience can experience, such as curiosity → unease → recognition →
relief. Attach each change to evidence: a sound before its source is visible, a gesture that
contradicts a line, or a reveal that changes the meaning of an earlier image. An emotion label
alone cannot direct a scene. Establish the ordinary pattern so a departure has something to change.

Give the sequence contrast in energy, proximity, density and sound. A quiet passage can allow
an implication to arrive; an intense passage can force attention onto an immediate problem.
Decide where the emotional peak belongs and what settles afterward. Avoid making every shot
equally grand or every performance equally emphatic. Earn a large gesture through what precedes it.

For [the courier example](CINEMATICS.md#worked-scene-a-courier-chooses-to-help), the emotional turn occurs when forward urgency becomes voluntary
hesitation, then commitment. Let the body, camera response and sound rhythm change around that
decision. The tram leaving gives the kindness a cost; the recipient's response gives it meaning.

### Direct subtext through behavior and relationships

Separate what a character wants, what they show and what they conceal. Someone reassuring a
friend might smooth a damaged ticket repeatedly while avoiding eye contact. Someone performing
confidence might claim the center of a room but retreat when another person enters. Choose
behavior that belongs to this character and situation; avoid a generic library of sadness gestures.

Stage relationships within the frame. Two people sharing an uninterrupted frame can invite
comparison of their responses; separating them can withhold that relationship. A doorway,
distance, a height difference or a prop between them can matter when the action makes it matter.
Let a listening character have a thought before answering. Allow a suppressed reaction to remain
small if the audience can see it. Do not add blinking, nodding or hand motion at regular intervals.

### Compose with masses, space, depth and movement

Begin with the large shapes and value groups. Identify the dominant subject, the secondary
information and the background. At thumbnail size, can the frame guide attention in the intended
order? Check silhouette, overlap and contrast before adding small detail. Rich construction and
surface craft still matter, but their placement should support the composition's hierarchy.

Use negative space deliberately. Empty space can leave room for an arrival, express a separation
or make an unseen direction feel important, depending on what the story has established. A
centered frame can feel formal, comic, calm or threatening in different contexts. A rule-of-thirds
grid is a framing aid, not an emotional instruction or a requirement to put every face off-center.

Arrange foreground, middle distance and background to express relationships and guide reveals.
An obstruction can make us feel we are watching from concealment; an open shared space can let
us study an interaction. Avoid a decorative foreground object that hides the decisive gesture.
Compose the movement too: how do shapes approach, separate, overlap and settle during the shot?
A beautiful first frame can develop into an unreadable image halfway through a move.

Plan the changing hierarchy with [CINEMATICS-ATTENTION.md](CINEMATICS-ATTENTION.md), then show
it in [boards and timed studies](CINEMATICS-STORYBOARDS.md). Optical sharpness, visual attention
and dramatic priority are separate decisions that should support this treatment.

### Give the world and recurring details meaning

Select environments, costumes, materials and props that reveal history, status, habits or pressure.
Choose a few details the camera/performance can use: an often-repaired latch, a carefully folded
letter, a chair nobody occupies. Follow the project's theme and asset quality bar; do not fill
every visible surface with equally emphatic ornament.

A motif is a recurring image, action or sound whose meaning can develop. Establish it clearly,
vary it when the story changes and make the return worth noticing. In the courier scene, an
early hand tightly gripping a ticket might return as an open hand helping another person. A
repeated gate composition could move from a promised escape to a departure willingly missed.
Use either if it fits the character; adding every possible symbol would weaken the scene's focus.

### Paint with light and color across the sequence

Use light to reveal, conceal, shape and separate. Decide what the audience can read in the face,
what stays in silhouette and where the eye goes next. Test soft versus hard transitions, light
direction, darkness and reflected light in the actual design. A silhouette may withhold identity;
it may also hide the expression the scene depends on. Make that trade deliberately.

Plan color relationships and their progression rather than assign fixed meanings to isolated hues.
What palette is familiar in this world? What accent has been associated with safety, authority or
danger by this film? Does a later use reinforce or overturn that association? Warm light can be
welcoming or oppressive; cool light can be tranquil or alienating. Context and contrast decide.
Compare key images in grayscale as well as color to see whether the value structure carries focus.

Disney's [lighting overview](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/lighting/) describes color
keys and visual treatments changing with story and emotion. Pixar's
[Soul cinematography account](https://renderman.pixar.com/stories/cinematography-with-soul)
connects photography references, tactile materials, lighting keys and early layout collaboration.
Use references and the particular character/material response to develop the look; judge skin,
fabric and metal under the intended light instead of applying one preset to all surfaces.

### Design the audience's listening perspective

Decide what the audience hears with special clarity and what recedes. The physical location of
a sound and its dramatic importance need not be identical: a distant bell can dominate someone's
attention, or a nearby conversation can become indistinct while they fixate on a small action.
Establish and transition that listening perspective so it feels intentional.

Use contrast between sound, image and expectation. A cheerful public announcement can continue
over a private disappointment; a room's familiar mechanical hum can stop before anything moves.
A musical arrival can affirm a decision, question it or be withheld entirely. Silence is designed
relative to the surrounding sound, not achieved automatically by muting every track. Listen to
whether the change serves the audience experience and preserves necessary information.

**Artistic review:** ask what the viewer felt, when that changed, which relationship or detail
they noticed, and what they expected next. Compare their answers with the treatment without
explaining the intended interpretation first. Mystery can be deliberate; an unintended reading
needs a specific revision. Keep the artistic choices that work, even when they depart from a convention.

## The same action, three artistic treatments

These are original alternatives to explore, not promises that a technique always produces an
emotion. Keep the basic events—approach, dropped parcel, choice, missed tram—and change how the
audience experiences them. Board and cut short versions before choosing the treatment.

| Intended experience | Composition, staging and performance | Rhythm, light and sound |
|---|---|---|
| Tender recognition | Begin with the courier moving alone through a busy space; let helping create a shared composition. Make the decision a small change in attention and weight; give the recipient time to respond. | Let the public rush continue while the interaction becomes the focus. Hold the response after departure; choose a readable, tactile look that belongs to the setting. |
| Suspense about the sacrifice | Establish the closing gate before the courier notices the parcel. Keep the competing routes legible and the handoff's progress visible; return to the courier when the available time becomes clear. | Build pressure through the established warning rhythm and approaching closure. Release it at the choice or consequence, depending on the intended ending; preserve enough quiet for the cost to register. |
| Comic frustration with a generous character | Set a precise expectation that the courier will just make it. Stage the interruption and delayed recognition cleanly in a frame that can hold the visual relationship; keep the reaction specific to the character. | Time the disruption against the established approach, then test a held reaction instead of another cut. Give the comic event sonic punctuation without burying it under constant music. |

Compare the versions on clarity, emotional effect, character consistency and fit with the rest
of the film. Do not select only by which still looks most impressive. Record why the chosen
treatment suits this story, then make the GraphCode assets follow that decision.

Keep the treatment beside [the shot record](CINEMATICS.md#keep-a-useful-shot-record). Review
its effect through the actual performance, edit and mix before calling the artistic choices settled.
