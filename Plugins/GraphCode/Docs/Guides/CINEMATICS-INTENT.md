# Define the presentation intent

Decide what the audience should experience before choosing the shots. A useful intent describes
a change in understanding, feeling, anticipation or attention, with something visible or audible
that can cause it. "Make it cinematic" and "show the environment" leave the directing decisions open.

Use [preproduction](CINEMATICS-PREPRODUCTION.md) to document the resulting plan,
[artistic direction](CINEMATICS-DIRECTION.md) to develop its treatment, and
[storyboards](CINEMATICS-STORYBOARDS.md) to work out how it unfolds.

## Separate purpose, treatment and execution

| Decision | What it determines | Example: courier at a tram stop |
|---|---|---|
| Presentation intent | What changes for this audience and why this sequence exists | Make a small act of kindness feel costly and voluntary. |
| Viewpoint and information | Whose experience guides us; what we know, discover or await | First share the courier's urgency; notice the stranger when the courier does. |
| Art direction and treatment | The visual, performance and sound language that expresses it | Worn everyday surroundings, restrained acting, patient observation at the decision. |
| Beat design | The meaningful progression of action and attention | Hurry → notice dropped belongings → hesitate → help → miss tram → receive acknowledgment. |
| Shot and edit design | The views, timing and connections that make those beats legible | Establish tram geography; hold the hesitation; reveal the departure in relation to the help. |
| Technical execution | How the chosen experience is built and verified | Sequence assets, bindings, performance, camera channels, sound placement and playback. |

These are related decisions, not interchangeable descriptions. A palette cannot supply a missing
dramatic turn. A dolly move cannot establish a product's function if its action is never visible.
When a choice changes, trace its consequences through the other rows.

## Write a brief another author can use

Save this in the [director's packet](CINEMATICS-PREPRODUCTION.md#save-the-directors-packet).
Keep it concise but specific enough to judge alternatives:

```text
Sequence / revision / delivery context:
Audience: prior knowledge, viewing context, intended next thought or action.
Purpose: why this passage belongs in the presentation.
Audience experience: starting understanding/feeling → intended ending state.
Subject and viewpoint: whose discovery, judgment or experience guides us?
Progression: opening promise, development, decisive change and payoff.
Evidence: what action, relationship, image or sound makes each change perceptible?
Revelation: what is known, withheld, discovered and deliberately unresolved?
Treatment: tone, performance, composition, light/color and listening perspective.
References: specific passages; what each teaches; what does not fit this project.
Delivery: format/aspect, duration target, playback context, sound and readability constraints.
Scope: required characters, locations, props, events, effects and production resources.
Success/failure: what a viewer should be able to perceive; what would undermine it.
Known decisions / assumptions / questions that need research or a planned study:
```

For character work, use playable actions: conceal, persuade, investigate, resist or protect.
"Hide disappointment until the other person leaves" directs behavior better than "look sad."
For a reveal, describe the progression of information and relationships. Do not add artificial
conflict to a presentation whose purpose is clear explanation, atmosphere or celebration.

A duration target belongs to delivery and scope. It is not a reason to pad an edit or give every
beat equal time. If a required action cannot read within the target, revise scope, staging or
structure before speeding everything up.

## Match the sequence to its presentation

The following are original planning examples, not fixed genres or mandatory structures:

| Presentation | Useful audience question | Possible progression | Evidence to preserve |
|---|---|---|---|
| Character scene | What choice did this person make, and what did it cost? | Want → pressure → decision → consequence | Behavior, reaction, relationship and the timing of the choice. |
| Environment reveal | Where am I, what makes this place distinctive, and what draws me onward? | Orientation → scale/character → meaningful detail → destination or mood payoff | Connected geography, relative scale, atmosphere and purposeful landmarks. |
| Product or mechanism demonstration | What is it, how does it work, and why is that useful? | Identity → operation → visible result → distinctive benefit | Unobscured interfaces, causal action, usable scale and a result the viewer can compare. |
| Trailer or announcement | What promise should make the audience want to know more? | Establish promise → develop contrast/stakes → memorable peak → invitation | A coherent premise, selective information and a payoff that fits the actual work. |
| In-game handoff | What changed, where am I, and what can I do next? | Orient → scripted event → consequence → playable opportunity | Player/target positions, actionable state and camera/control recovery. |
| Atmospheric or associative passage | What sensory idea or emotional relationship should accumulate? | Establish a motif → vary it → transform or resolve it | Deliberate image/sound associations and contrast across the passage. |

An environment fly-through may need a destination and changing scale; a mechanism demonstration
may need a stable explanatory view. Neither becomes stronger simply by borrowing suspense cuts
or shallow focus from a character scene. Select the language that serves the presentation.

### Account for the viewing situation

Decide whether the sequence is watched from a fixed screen, interrupted by interaction, played
silently in a feed, or presented with a reliable soundtrack. Identify the actual deliverables,
aspect ratios and relevant display conditions. Test important detail at the intended size.
Plan alternate framing when a delivery crop would remove a key relationship or caption.

For in-game work, define what the player knows before entry and what they inherit afterward.
A cinematic that ends on a beautiful distant landmark may still fail to orient the player toward
the usable exit. Follow [GraphCode integration](CINEMATICS-GRAPHCODE.md) for runtime proof.

## Choose the audience's knowledge deliberately

Write the knowledge change beside each beat:

- **Shared discovery:** viewer and character notice together. Preserve a clear trigger and the
  character's response; an early establishing shot must not reveal what should be discovered later.
- **Audience ahead:** show something the character does not know. Build anticipation from that
  difference; keep geography and timing understandable enough for the audience to anticipate it.
- **Character ahead:** conceal the character's knowledge or intention. Provide fair visible behavior
  and a later payoff; confusion about basic action is not automatically productive mystery.
- **Observational viewpoint:** let the audience compare people or events without adopting one
  person's literal view. Use staging and timing to make that comparison available.

A literal point-of-view shot is one technique for viewpoint. A listener's reaction, what the edit
omits, camera distance and sound perspective also determine whose experience guides the scene.
Record intentional viewpoint changes and the beat that motivates them.

## Turn intent into choices you can test

Use this chain for a consequential choice:

```text
Intended effect → necessary evidence → artistic choice → practical construction → review
```

Original example: "The courier knowingly sacrifices the tram" needs the help and departure to
have a readable temporal relationship. A shared frame with the tram in depth is one candidate;
a reaction and clearly connected departure cut is another. Test whether the gesture, hesitation
and departure read at speed. A dramatic low angle alone cannot establish the sacrifice.

Original environment example: "The workshop is small but ingenious" needs spatial scale and
working details in relationship. Start with a human-scale entrance and reveal the linked mechanisms
through purposeful staging. Compare a held reveal with a pan that follows the mechanism's operation.
Reject a pass that shows many objects but makes their function or scale impossible to read.

Original product example: "The latch can be operated with one occupied hand" needs the occupied
hand, free hand, latch travel and successful opening. Plan a view with readable contacts and an
unobstructed result. A beauty orbit may belong before or after that proof; test the demonstration
as an action rather than judge its hero frame alone.

For movement and transitions, name the change they express. "Pan when the courier hears the
stranger" is actionable once trigger, subject, passage and landing are planned. "Use a dissolve
because it is cinematic" does not establish a reason. Use the
[movement](CINEMATICS-CAMERA-MOVEMENT.md) and
[transition](CINEMATICS-EDITING-AND-TRANSITIONS.md) chapters for selection and execution.

Use [CINEMATICS-TECHNIQUE-SELECTION.md](CINEMATICS-TECHNIQUE-SELECTION.md) to compare complete
constructions for intimacy, suspense, comedy, scale, explanation and other presentations. Record
the intended change, necessary evidence, selected technique and reason before production.

## Define focus at three levels

**Dramatic focus** is the thought, action or relationship that matters now. **Visual attention**
is where and in what order the image directs the eye. **Optical focus** is the camera's plane
of sharpness and its transition. Define them separately, then coordinate them with sound.

The important action might be a hand deciding to release a ticket; its shape, timing, gaze cue
and surrounding contrast must make it available. Making the hand sharp cannot solve every other
attention problem. Use [audience attention](CINEMATICS-ATTENTION.md) to plan and diagnose the result.

## Review the brief before production planning

Can another author describe the audience's intended change, its evidence and its fit with the
project's art direction? Can they distinguish essential beats from optional embellishment?
Do the delivery constraints and available resources support that experience?

If the answer depends on seeing the space, write a
[rough view-study question](CINEMATICS-STORYBOARDS.md#run-simple-view-studies-before-selecting-final-shots).
Record the result and update the brief where needed. Continue through the director's packet;
do not treat a good brief or an appealing study as a completed plan.
