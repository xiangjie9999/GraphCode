# Design cinematic shots and coverage

Choose a frame for what the audience needs to perceive, then stage the action that makes it
meaningful. This chapter covers shot sizes, viewpoints, blocking, geography, coverage and lens
choices. Start with [the workflow](CINEMATICS.md) and [artistic direction](CINEMATICS-DIRECTION.md).
Use [camera movement](CINEMATICS-CAMERA-MOVEMENT.md) for executing a move and
[editing/transitions](CINEMATICS-EDITING-AND-TRANSITIONS.md) for joining the resulting shots.

Save the [director's packet](CINEMATICS-PREPRODUCTION.md) before production. Use
[rough view studies](CINEMATICS-STORYBOARDS.md#run-simple-view-studies-before-selecting-final-shots)
to compare uncertain framings or short passes with written criteria; record why the selected
view serves the intent, then carry it into boards, timing and shot records.

## Choose framing and viewpoint

Shot size describes how much of the subject and its surroundings we see. It is not a fixed focal
length: subject size, camera distance, filmback and lens all contribute. Frame for the information
and behavior that must read at the intended viewing size.

Common labels are approximate: a full shot includes the figure; a medium often includes the
torso and hands; a medium close-up tightens toward chest/shoulders and face; a close-up isolates
the face or another important subject; an extreme close-up isolates a smaller detail. Decide
the actual boundaries from the performance, body/prop proportions and output aspect ratio.

| Framing | Choose it when | Execute and review |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme wide / establishing view | Location, scale, paths or a person's relationship to the world matters now. | Give geography recognizable landmarks and an attention hierarchy. Check that the subject/action remains discoverable; scale alone may hide the story. |
| Wide / full figure | The audience must read bodies, travel, physical comedy, contact or several people acting together. | Leave usable space for the action, including its consequence. Do not cut off feet when support or footwork carries the beat. |
| Medium | Gesture, face and nearby interaction need to read together. | Stage hands/props at a readable height and depth. Check that a useful gesture does not leave the frame. |
| Medium close-up | A face needs emphasis while shoulders/posture still help express the thought. | Keep the useful body response; compare whether a tighter close-up would lose it or a medium would give needed gesture room. |
| Close-up | A thought, withheld response or small change of attention deserves concentration. | Give the performer a playable intention and enough time to process it. Keep the decisive eye/face detail readable through focus and motion. |
| Extreme close-up / insert | A specific detail supplies necessary information or a motif: a latch engaging, a damaged ticket, a hand releasing. | Establish whose object/action it is and what changes. Match hand, orientation, action phase and prop state with the surrounding coverage. |
| Two-shot / group frame | Comparison, shared space, separation or a changing relationship matters. | Compose the relationship through the whole action. Let both listening and speaking exist; avoid isolating every line automatically. |
| Over-the-shoulder | The other person's physical presence shapes an exchange. | Choose how much foreground person belongs in the image. Check eyeline, obstruction and foreground prominence; an oversized blurred shoulder can hide the actual reaction. |
| POV / near-POV | The viewer should share someone's access to information or attention. | Establish the look/position, then show a believable view of its target. A near-POV may preserve a shoulder or offset; do not claim literal eyesight when the camera has incompatible access. |

Establish a baseline for camera height and angle, then vary it for a reason. An eye-level view
can keep us in an encounter; a high or low position can reveal a spatial relationship, change
access to a face or make scale important. An overhead can explain a route but remove facial
access. A canted horizon (Dutch angle) introduces a conspicuous imbalance. None of these has one
guaranteed emotional meaning; judge it against the character, action and surrounding frames.

For a conversation, first stage the entire exchange in a shared frame. Identify the moments when
listening, withholding, interruption or a changing alliance requires different coverage. Reverse
angles should respect the chosen geography and gaze targets. A close-up is earned by what it
lets us perceive, rather than by the fact that the character has started speaking.

For action, establish the goal, threat and relevant space before fragments become necessary.
Choose wide coverage for a trajectory/contact and closer coverage for a decision or crucial
detail. Preserve the causal sequence: preparation → action → result → response. Withholding a
part can create suspense, but the eventual reveal must let the viewer understand what happened.

## Stage the complete action in 3D

Block the actors, props and important environmental events through the scene. Choose positions
and movement that express relationships: who approaches, withdraws, obstructs, notices or acts.
Rehearse this with rough poses and timing. Preserve room for a discovery during layout to improve
the boards. Use reference performance or mocap deliberately and adapt it to the actual character,
space, prop and camera.

Draw a simple overhead plan showing entrances, exits, action paths, eyeline targets and the
important axis of action. Establish where things are when that knowledge matters. A landmark,
two-shot or motivated movement can do this; an opening aerial is not compulsory. Mark prop hand,
contact points, gaze target and event order at each important beat.

Review silhouettes, overlaps, occlusion and background tangencies from the intended camera.
Stage important information where the audience can notice it. If a character must read a label,
consider its actual screen size, light, motion and time on screen. Do not assume an asset's
existence makes its meaning visible.

ASC's [camera-placement discussion](https://theasc.com/article/shot-craft-where-do-you-put-the-camera/)
connects camera choice to emotional viewpoint, blocking, geography and reactions. Use rehearsal
to choose coverage; be willing to improve the planned shot when the performance suggests it.

**Review:** the rough scene communicates action and relationships in continuous playback. Resolve
staging before dressing every corner of the set. Proxies must be sufficient to judge scale,
occlusion and motion; final presentation still needs the required asset quality.

## Design coverage and the audience's attention

Every proposed shot should answer four questions:

1. What should the viewer attend to now?
2. What does this framing reveal, withhold or make them feel?
3. What changes during the shot, including a change inside a hold?
4. Why do we enter and leave at these moments?

A wide view can show relationships; a close view can isolate a decision or meaningful detail.
A reaction can change how an event is interpreted. Choose coverage for those functions. Shooting
each subject at several sizes without an editorial purpose leaves the real design until later.

Plan where attention lands at the end of one shot and where the next shot asks it to go. Guide
that transfer with position, movement, gaze, contrast and sound. Inspect the result at playback
speed; a technically matching cut can still make the viewer search for the subject. Sometimes
that search or disorientation is intentional—record what it achieves and test whether it reads.

Maintain a usable sense of direction and time. In conventional continuity, keep screen direction,
eyelines, action phase and prop state consistent. If changing sides of an action axis, establish
the change through staging, a visible move or new geography as appropriate. These conventions
help comprehension; they are not prohibitions against expressive discontinuity. Check the effect
on the viewer instead of mechanically enforcing an angle rule.

For action, make the source, trajectory and consequence legible. For dialogue, stage listening,
interruption and changing relationships as carefully as speech. For a reveal, control the order
in which silhouette, scale, context and detail become known. Let the most important moment have
space; constant visual intensity removes useful contrast.

## Choose camera position, lens and focus

Start from viewpoint and staging, then solve the frame. Use a consistent filmback and output
aspect ratio unless the design calls for a change. Record camera height, distance, angle, focal
length, focus and aperture where they affect the shot. Evaluate them as a combination.

Perspective comes from the camera's relationship to the scene. Changing focal length from one
position changes the view; moving to restore the same subject size changes spatial relationships.
A dolly changes parallax in a way a zoom does not. Distinguish that from geometric lens distortion.
See ASC's [lens-distortion explanation](https://theasc.com/article/understanding-lens-distortion/).
In Unreal, the [Cine Camera Actor](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/cinematic-cameras-in-unreal-engine?application_version=5.7)
provides filmback, lens, focus and post-process controls; inspect their current properties before
authoring them.

Use focus to make attention readable. Mark who or what must be sharp at each beat, and rehearse
a focus pull with the performance. Avoid losing the meaningful reaction while another object
crosses the frame. Aperture, distance and focal length all influence the useful depth of field;
shallow focus, blur, flare, shake, grain or letterboxing alone do not establish cinematic quality.

For a move, rehearse the complete path using [the movement guide](CINEMATICS-CAMERA-MOVEMENT.md).
For focus transfer, see its [rack-focus procedure](CINEMATICS-CAMERA-MOVEMENT.md#rack-focus).

## Example: choose coverage for a dilemma

Use the courier dilemma in [CINEMATICS.md](CINEMATICS.md#worked-scene-a-courier-chooses-to-help).
Keep the same story event and compare these original choices before authoring final assets:

| Moment | Choice to try | Why and what must read |
|---|---|---|
| Approach | Side tracking shot with the gate ahead | Share forward progress and establish the goal. Preserve enough geography to understand the later cost. |
| Parcel falls | Reactive pan from courier to parcel | Let a cue redirect attention in shared space. The landing must identify the need; avoid revealing the parcel before the cue. |
| Recognition | Cut back to a medium/close reaction | Show the thought between noticing and deciding. Give the gaze toward the gate a clear target. |
| Choice | Hold a shared frame, or make a restrained push | Let the changed weight/direction establish agency. Choose the push only if its timing deepens that decision. |
| Handoff | Match on action if a new view is needed | Preserve reach, contact, support and release. Keep only one audible handoff event. |
| Missed tram | Let departure sound continue over the response | An L cut can carry the cost while we watch the human result. Do not let the sound mask a quiet reply. |
| Later passage | Cut directly, use a graphic motif, or dissolve | Choose the intended time/idea relationship. A changed hand gesture may connect meaning; a dissolve needs useful overlapping images/handles. |

For suspense, try showing the closing gate before the courier notices the parcel. For gentleness,
try holding the shared interaction while the tram leaves off-screen. For comedy, try a planted
frame that lets the well-established route fail at the last moment. Evaluate each treatment's
clarity, character and rhythm; no single technique is the professional choice in every version.

## Review the coverage

Play the complete action and its neighboring cuts at delivery speed. Check what becomes known,
whose response matters, where the viewer looks and whether every essential action can be read.
Inspect silhouettes, gaze, support, hand/prop state, subject size and continuity across the edit.
Keep decisions in [the shot record](CINEMATICS.md#keep-a-useful-shot-record).

For practice, block one exchange in a shared frame, then add only coverage that changes the
audience's access to performance or information. Compare a version built around the listener
with one built around the speaker; explain why the chosen viewpoint fits the scene.
