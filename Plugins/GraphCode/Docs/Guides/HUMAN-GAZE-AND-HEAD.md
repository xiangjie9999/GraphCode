# Human movement: gaze and head

[Human movement guides](HUMAN-MOVEMENT.md) · this page: looking, moving the head, and coordinating attention with action.

A convincing person looks toward the next useful place, turns the head when the task needs it, and keeps the view
steady while the body moves. Eyes, head and trunk do not share one rotation curve. During a large gaze shift the eyes
first move toward the target, then turn back within the head as the head finishes moving.
([Barnes, 1979, acquiring visual targets][Barnes79], abstract.)

**What a correct-looking result is.** Small glances can use the eyes alone. A walking turn directs attention toward
the future path. Before handling an object, gaze finds the object or contact point. A walking head compensates for
body motion. Conversation includes looking away, and blink behaviour changes with the task. These are patterns to
check against the tables below, not a universal personality preset.

**Read the numbers correctly.** `C` means full text read in the retained research; `Ca`, abstract only; `Cs`, a named
secondary source; `D`, arithmetic on cited values, with the formula shown. Mean ± SD and mean ± SEM stay distinct.
Angles are anatomical unless a row gives another convention. Eye-in-head, head-in-space and head-on-trunk are
different measurements. A range is a limit, not a pose target. Observed habits and maximal-effort trials do not
define comfortable everyday motion. These sources were recorded on 2026-10-07; they have not been fetched again.

## The movement, in order

### Shift attention to a visible target

| Phase | What moves | Timing and evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Acquire the target | Eyes saccade toward it; the head may join | In humans viewing a visual target, head acceleration starts **14 ms (SD 59) before** eye-saccade onset on average. The eye often starts first for offsets **under 45°**. In darkness the reported lag is **108 ms (SD 85)**. The retained abstract does not resolve that lag's sign. [Barnes, 1979, acquiring visual targets][Barnes79] (`Ca`) |
| Redirect gaze | The eye movement finishes quickly in ordinary saccades; a large head-eye turn can take longer | Two secondary fits are **21 + 2.2 × amplitude ms** and **37 + 2.7 × amplitude ms**, with amplitude in degrees. [Carpenter, 1988, saccade duration, via ar5iv][Carpenter88] and [Baloh et al., saccade duration, via Duchowski, 2017][Duchowski17] (`Cs`; Baloh year not retained) |
| Stabilize the new view | Eyes counter-rotate as the head continues | The slower return toward the centre of the orbit cancels remaining head motion. No human duration for this phase is supplied. [Barnes, 1979, acquiring visual targets][Barnes79] (`Ca`) |
| Explore a scene | Gaze and head continue according to the task | While viewing **180° images**, head follows gaze by a median **211 ms**; gaze is a median **1.175 times** farther from straight ahead. A separate study reports an average **58 ms** delay through the same secondary source. [Kangas et al., 2022, gaze and head in image viewing][Kangas22] (`C`); [Sitzmann et al., 2018, via Kangas, 2022][Kangas22] (`Cs`) |

The image-viewing delays are not the onset delays of a single ordinary glance. Do not force them onto every shift.
For walking turns, Kim reports eyes, then head, then trunk. Hollands reports simultaneous eye and head onset on
average in a different cued task. Keep the task with its timing.
([Kim, Fricke & Bock, 2021, gaze during walking turns][Kim21], `Ca`;
[Hollands, Patla & Vickers, 2002, look where you're going][Hollands02], `Ca`.)

### Look, then handle an object

| Phase | Timing | Task and evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Look at the object | First look **0.56 s** before handling starts | Tea making. [Land, Mennie & Rusted, 1999, gaze in tea making][Land99] (`Ca`) |
| Start the reach | Eye movement starts **40–100 ms** before hand movement | Eye and hand onset, not fixation-to-contact time. [Desanghere & Marotta, 2015, eye–hand coordination][Desanghere15] (`Cs`, citing Prablanc 1979, Biguer 1982 and Land 1999) |
| Coordinate eyes, head and arm | Saccade lands about **150 ms** later; head starts with the eyes; arm speeds up about **130 ms** after the head; forearm muscles fire **80 ms** after arm onset | **30** able-bodied people, **14** amputees; **9,703** able-bodied trials. Preserve each event's own origin. [Gregori et al., 2019, reach-to-grasp coordination][Gregori19] (`C`) |
| Reach the object | Hand covers the object about **500 ms** after the first fixation; median fixation-to-grasp **561 ms** | Same study. [Gregori et al., 2019, reach-to-grasp coordination][Gregori19] (`C`) |
| Move attention onward | Gaze moves to the next object **0.61 s** before the current act finishes. One act lasts **3.3 s**, with about **7 fixations** | Tea making. [Land, Mennie & Rusted, 1999, gaze in tea making][Land99] (`Ca`) |

In the bar-to-switch task, gaze fixates the grasp site, target and support surface, rather than the hand or moving
object. Gaze leaves each point when the event there happens. This is a task result, not a ban on ever looking at a
hand. ([Johansson, Westling, Bäckström & Flanagan, 2001, gaze and manipulation][Johansson01], `Ca`.)

The **40–100 ms** onset lead and the **0.56 s / 561 ms** fixation leads measure different intervals. They do not
conflict. Their sources and timing anchors are in the table; do not average them.

### Blink

| Phase | Timing or distance | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Close | **0.10 ± 0.02 s** | Spontaneous blinks, **8 people**. [Casse et al., 2007, blink kinematics][Casse07] (`Ca`) |
| Remain closed | No duration supplied | The phase is identified for voluntary blinks. [Kwon et al., 2013, voluntary eye blinking kinematics][Kwon13] (`Ca`) |
| Open | **0.21 ± 0.04 s**, longer than closing | Spontaneous blinks. [Casse et al., 2007, blink kinematics][Casse07] (`Ca`) |
| Complete the lid travel | Vertical **9.1 ± 2.2 mm** spontaneous, **12.3 ± 1.9 mm** voluntary; horizontal **2.7 ± 0.9 mm** and **3.8 ± 0.9 mm** | Same study. [Casse et al., 2007, blink kinematics][Casse07] (`Ca`) |

`D`: **0.31 s** = **0.10 s + 0.21 s**, using Casse's mean closing and opening times. Closed time is excluded.
Kwon divides reopening into early and late phases but supplies no separate durations in the retained abstract.
Do not combine a voluntary phase description with spontaneous measurements and call it a measured full blink.

## Angles, distances and speeds

### How much the eyes and head turn

| Measure | Value and convention | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Eye-only range | Width **35.8 ± 31.9°**; customary ocular motor range, where the eye ends, **44.0 ± 23.8°**. Head amplitude then rises with required eye eccentricity, slope **0.77 ± 0.16** | Horizontal centrifugal saccades to an LED ring; strongly different between people. [Stahl, 1999, eye-only range][Stahl99] (`Ca`) |
| Eye-only range in another task | About **±18° on average**. **90 %** of shifts without head movement are within **±30° horizontally** or **±12° vertically** | **7 people**, **360° display**. [Fang et al., 2015, eye and head contribution][Fang15] (`C`) |
| Head contribution | Slopes **0.23 horizontal / 0.11 vertical** for one saccade per head movement; **0.44 / 0.30** for three saccades | Same study; axes not defined in the retained text. These are not a directly usable head-share curve. [Fang et al., 2015, eye and head contribution][Fang15] (`C`) |
| Human oculomotor range | About **±55°** | [Guitton & Volle, 1987, oculomotor range][Guitton87] (`Ca`) |
| Human and rhesus range in a review | About **±40°**; rhesus mechanical limit **±45°** | Mixed-species review; the rhesus limit is not a human rig limit. [Freedman, 2008, eye–head coordination][Freedman08] (`C`) |
| Head-braked human gaze shifts | Under about **45°**, eye alone; beyond that, eye saccade averages about **45°**, short of the full range. Eye plus head movements reach gaze shifts up to **160°** | The faster the head, the smaller the eye saccade. [Guitton & Volle, 1987, oculomotor range][Guitton87] (`Ca`) |
| Fast whole-body turns | Eye saccade **46.8 ± 5.6°**, approaching the mechanical eye-in-orbit limit | **6 people**, turns in darkness as fast as possible. [Anastasopoulos et al., 2015, whole-body gaze shifts][Anastasopoulos15] (`C`) |

**Keep the disagreements.** Stahl's **35.8°** is a width; Fang's **±18°** is a half range. The records do not establish
that they are interchangeable. Likewise **±40°**, **±55°**, and a head-braked saccade of about **45°** describe different
conditions. None is a universal point where every person must turn the head. Sources are in the corresponding rows.

### Saccade timing and speed

| Measure | Value | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Duration examples | At **10°**, **43 ms** (Carpenter) and **64 ms** (Baloh); at **30°**, **87 ms** and **118 ms** | `D`: insert amplitude into the two formulas in the phase table. [Carpenter, 1988, via ar5iv][Carpenter88]; [Baloh et al., via Duchowski, 2017][Duchowski17] |
| Duration fit range | Linear with amplitude up to **50°**, steeper beyond | **3 people**, scleral coil. [Collewijn, Erkelens & Steinman, 1988, horizontal saccades][Collewijn88] (`Ca`) |
| Accuracy | About **0.5°** undershoot, independent of amplitude over **10–70°** | Between continuously visible targets. [Collewijn, Erkelens & Steinman, 1988, horizontal saccades][Collewijn88] (`Ca`) |
| Peak speed ceiling | **502 ± 32°/s** for saccades of **40° and more** | Same study. [Collewijn, Erkelens & Steinman, 1988, horizontal saccades][Collewijn88] (`Ca`) |
| Other peak speed fits | **551°/s** for the Baloh fit; **895.06°/s** for Duchowski's own fit; microsaccades **236.22°/s** | Different fits and movement class; do not average. [Duchowski et al., 2017, saccade velocity fits][Duchowski17] (`Cs` in the retained research) |
| Eye movement inside a fast whole-body turn | **448 ± 135°/s**, duration **261 ± 87 ms**, amplitude **46.8 ± 5.6°** | This is not an ordinary eye-only saccade. [Anastasopoulos et al., 2015, whole-body gaze shifts][Anastasopoulos15] (`C`) |

`D`: the **21 + 2.2A** fit gives **124 ms** at **46.8°** (rounded as in the retained calculation).
The whole-body-turn eye movement above is about twice as long. Keep that task distinction.

### Head speed and total gaze transfer in a fast turn

These are maximal-speed whole-body turns in darkness, not everyday head-only turns. Head speed is in space;
gaze-transfer duration is the whole transfer, not the time for the head alone.
([Anastasopoulos et al., 2015, whole-body gaze shifts][Anastasopoulos15], `C`.)

| Target turn | Single-step head peak | Multi-step head peak | Single-step gaze transfer | Multi-step gaze transfer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **90°** | **279 ± 80°/s** | **172 ± 52°/s** | **315 ± 75 ms** | **506 ± 78 ms** |
| **135°** | **363 ± 110°/s** | **211 ± 64°/s** | **449 ± 107 ms** | **611 ± 132 ms** |
| **180°** | **428 ± 127°/s** | **266 ± 54°/s** | **599 ± 135 ms** | **791 ± 138 ms** |

The corresponding final gaze amplitudes are **84.6 ± 5.0°**, **131.6 ± 8.1°**, and **172.1 ± 9.8°**.
Leg muscles begin about **30 ms before** the eyes; biceps femoris and lateral oblique begin **40–50 ms after** them.
Head-in-space velocity is a smooth bell curve and peaks before gaze transfer ends.
([Anastasopoulos et al., 2015, whole-body gaze shifts][Anastasopoulos15], `C`.)

### The head while walking

| Phase or condition | Angle, distance or timing | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Locomotion with the view stabilized | Head pitch amplitude, measured with the Frankfort plane, **not above 20°** across walking, walking in place, running in place and hopping | **10 people**. The plane stays around earth horizontal; darkness tilts it down. This is not a neck range. [Pozzo et al., 1990, head stabilization][Pozzo90] (`Ca`) |
| Those four tasks | Vertical head travel **1 to 25 cm**; frequencies **0.4–3.5 Hz**, harmonics to **6–8 Hz** | The entire range spans different tasks. It is not a walking bob target. [Pozzo et al., 1990, head stabilization][Pozzo90] (`Ca`) |
| Walking at **0.6–1.2 m/s** | Little head pitch in space; head-on-trunk cancels trunk pitch | Treadmill, ages **26–44**, tested **0.6–2.2 m/s**. [Hirasaki et al., 1999, walking velocity and head motion][Hirasaki99] (`Ca`) |
| Walking above **1.2 m/s** | Head translation grows and head pitch compensates; best coordination at **1.2–1.8 m/s** | Same study. [Hirasaki et al., 1999, walking velocity and head motion][Hirasaki99] (`Ca`) |
| Head-motion frequency | **1.4 Hz at 0.6 m/s** to **2.5 Hz at 2.2 m/s**; about **2.0 Hz across 0.8–1.8 m/s** | People lengthen the stride rather than step faster in that middle range. [Hirasaki et al., 1999, walking velocity and head motion][Hirasaki99] (`Ca`) |
| Forward head axis | Aims about **1 m** ahead at **1.67 m/s** | **5 people**. This is head fixation distance, not the distance of every eye fixation. [Moore et al., 1999, head and eye motion in walking][Moore99] (`Ca`) |
| Eye compensation | Far target **2 m**: eye velocity opposite head pitch, gain **0.8**. Near target **0.25 m**: eye in phase, about **three times** as fast | Same study. [Moore et al., 1999, head and eye motion in walking][Moore99] (`Ca`) |
| Walking at **100 m/min** | Peak head pitch and yaw speed about **17°/s** | [Moore et al., 2001, locomotor head motion][Moore01] (`Ca`) |
| Walking or running | Group median maximal head speed **not above 90°/s** | **20 people**. Vigorous voluntary yaw instead reaches a group median maximal **780°/s**. These are different tasks. [Grossman et al., 1988, head perturbations during locomotion][Grossman88] (`Ca`) |

### Where to look along the path

| Task | Gaze target and timing | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Flat, medium and rough terrain | Look-ahead about **1.5 s**. Flat: looking down about **half the time**. Medium and rough: ground viewed **more than 90 %** of the time | Medium: mostly **two steps** ahead; rough: split between **two and three steps**. [Matthis et al., 2018, foot placement, via Futurity][Matthis18] (`Cs`) |
| Stepping targets | Travel gaze **over 50 %** of the time; landing targets fixated about **two steps** ahead, **800–1000 ms** before landing | [Patla & Vickers, 2003, how far ahead do we look][Patla03] (`Ca`) |
| An obstacle | Obstacle fixated about **20 %** of approach time; no fixation while stepping over in this task | Planning occurs in preceding steps. [Patla & Vickers, 1997, stepping over an obstacle][Patla97] (`Ca`) |
| Stairs | Most time on the central stairs about **3 steps** ahead; shorter fixations descending | **10 young + 10 older people**. [Zietz & Hollands, 2009, gaze on stairs][Zietz09] (`Ca`) |
| Stairs, another study | Stair features fixated **under 20 %** of the time; gaze **within 4 steps** ahead; handrail rarely fixated | **11 people**, **7 steps**. [Miyasike-daSilva et al., 2011, stair gaze][Miyasike11] (`Ca`) |

The stair studies give a typical location and a bound, respectively. They do not require the gaze to jump to one
fixed tread at every step. For feet and rails, use [HUMAN-STAIRS-AND-LADDERS.md](HUMAN-STAIRS-AND-LADDERS.md).

### Lead a walking turn

| Event or condition | Lead, angle or distance | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Prepare for a corner | Head and eyes point toward the future path about **1 s ahead**, even in darkness | **6 adults**, **90° corners**. [Grasso et al., 1998, eye-head coordination for steering][Grasso98] (`Ca`) |
| Start head yaw before body yaw | About **200 ms** | Walking a curved path. [Courtine & Schieppati, 2003, human walking along a curved path][Courtine03] (`Ca`) |
| Begin turning the head before an obstacle | About **1.1 m before** it, for **45–135° turns**; speed falls with increasing turn angle | Same onset distance across those turns. [Sreenivasa et al., 2008, curved paths of different angles][Sreenivasa08] (`Ca`) |
| Enter a **90° turn** | Eyes **−407 ± 116 ms**, head **−50 ± 53 ms**, relative to first inside-foot contact; trunk, then pelvis follow | **10 young adults**, self-paced; **mean ± SEM**. Onset is yaw-velocity minimum before the peak. [Baker et al., 2020, segment coordination in turning][Baker20] (`C`, results / Fig 2) |
| Cued turn | Eye saccade and head turn start together on average; gaze stays on goal until head alignment | **30° or 60°**, cue one step before. [Hollands, Patla & Vickers, 2002, look where you're going][Hollands02] (`Ca`) |
| Corner curvature | **50 cm** radius in **2 steps**, or **200 cm** in **5–7 steps**; about **0.4 g** sideways | **5 people**. Head roll up to **8°** before the turn; head yaw leads the path change by up to **25°**; trunk does not anticipate. [Imai et al., 2001, body, head and eyes during turning][Imai01] (`Ca`) |

Corner arrival, body yaw, obstacle distance and inside-foot contact are different timing anchors. These leads are
not interchangeable delays. For the walking mechanics, follow [WALK-AND-RUN.md](WALK-AND-RUN.md) and
[HUMAN-TURNING.md](HUMAN-TURNING.md).

### Blinking and conversation

| State or event | Value | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Blink rate by task | Rest **17/min**, talking **26/min**, reading **4.5/min** | **150 people**. Task changes the rate. [Bentivoglio et al., 1997, blink rate by task][Bentivoglio97] (`Ca`) |
| Spontaneous blink rate in another lab | **18.4 ± 3.4 per minute** | **8 people**. [Casse et al., 2007, blink kinematics][Casse07] (`Ca`) |
| Blink with a large gaze shift | Lid-closing muscle fires with **97 %** of shifts larger than **33°**; blink typically begins with head or eye movement | Muscle firing is the reported measure. [Evinger et al., 1994, not looking while leaping][Evinger94] (`Ca`) |
| Looking within a face | Speaking: eyes **48 %**, mouth **29 %**. Listening: eyes **43 %**, mouth **34 %** | Percentages are conditional on looking at the face. **49 people**, **107 conversation sets**, **4-minute chats**. [Rogers et al., 2018, conversation gaze][Rogers18] (`C`) |
| Mutual face gaze | **63 % (SD 16)** of conversation, episodes averaging **2.2 s** | Same study. [Rogers et al., 2018, conversation gaze][Rogers18] (`C`) |
| Mutual eye contact | **12 % (SD 12)** of conversation, episodes averaging **0.36 s** | Same study; eye contact and face gaze are different. [Rogers et al., 2018, conversation gaze][Rogers18] (`C`) |
| End a speaking turn | Speaker ends with direct gaze; listener begins speaking with gaze averted | **19 pairs**. [Ho, Foulsham & Kingstone, 2015, gaze and conversational turns][Ho15] (`Ca`) |
| Gaze and the next speaker | Partner speech starts about **400 ms** after gaze onset | [Ho, Foulsham & Kingstone, 2015, gaze and conversational turns][Ho15] (`C`). The retained record does not assign the more detailed timing variants to their games |

Rates and percentages describe groups. They do not give evenly spaced blink intervals, compulsory eye contact at a
fixed period, or a head-nod amplitude.

## The most common mistakes

These checks are inferences from the cited observations, not separately measured error rates.

1. **Every small glance turns the head equally.** Eye-only shifts are common, with wide differences between people.
   Use the eye-only table; do not make its mean a hard switch. [Fang et al., 2015, eye and head contribution][Fang15].
2. **Eyes remain locked in the head through a large turn.** They counter-rotate while the head finishes moving.
   [Barnes, 1979, acquiring visual targets][Barnes79].
3. **A walking head copies every trunk pitch, or is completely rigid.** Head-on-trunk movement compensates for the
   body. [Hirasaki et al., 1999, walking velocity and head motion][Hirasaki99].
4. **Gaze watches the moving hand throughout handling.** The manipulation task directs gaze to contact points.
   [Johansson et al., 2001, gaze and manipulation][Johansson01].
5. **Reading and talking have the same metronomic blink.** Task rates differ, and no interval distribution is supplied.
   [Bentivoglio et al., 1997, blink rate by task][Bentivoglio97].
6. **Conversational partners keep continuous eye contact.** Mutual face gaze occupies much more time than mutual eye
   contact. [Rogers et al., 2018, conversation gaze][Rogers18].

## How to author it in GraphCode

[ANIMATION.md](ANIMATION.md#natural-movement-is-part-of-the-performance) owns attention, expression and native
face/body playback. [RIG.md](RIG.md) owns calibrated joints and controls. [POSES.md](POSES.md) owns local motion,
constraints and timing. Use those mechanics; this guide supplies the human reference.

| You need | Documented authoring route | Limit to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Authored head rotation | `swing(bone, axis=..., by=...)` with the actual bone and measured axis; use a `profile` for timing | No universal head bone, axis sign or neck range is supplied here. Head-in-space data must account for trunk rotation |
| A head directed at a target | `aim(chain, at=$PointOrFrame, weight=...)` on an actual declared chain, or with no rig chain on a `bones()` group: `Look = bones(neck_01, neck_02, head)`, `Face = frame(bone=head, toward=+Y, up=+X)`, `aim($Look, from=$Face, at=$Opponent) [share=(0.45, neck_01=0.1)]`, or one bone, `aim(head, ...)` (2026-10-14) | The ordinary aim convention is bone +X; on a MetaHuman the face is the head bone's +Y, so aim `from=` a face frame. `[share=]` splits the head-on-neck turn as you state it (each bone as far as its range lets it; a stopped bone's share goes to the rest); it does not move the eyes or the chest: give the chest its own swing and the eyes their face curves. An aim holds the bones above its group still while other reaches are solved (before 2026-10-14 it let the spine turn and the look ended short) |
| Eye or lid articulation | A verified rig control through `drive(control, by=...)`, or the actual native face rig and playback | The guides show no dedicated eye, gaze, saccade or blink instruction. A control name is authored; a float curve needs a verified consumer |
| Different event onsets | Separate profiles or documented `at(frame=..., arrive=...)` controls | Preserve the source's timing origin. [ANIMATION.md](ANIMATION.md#compose-movement-in-stages) says control frame numbers refer to the original clip |
| Gaze coordinated with a reach | Stage attention before the hand action; follow [HANDS.md](HANDS.md) and [HUMAN-GRASP-AND-REACH.md](HUMAN-GRASP-AND-REACH.md) | Hand IK does not infer attention or choose the next object |
| Head motion over walking | Compose the head performance with the walk from [WALK-AND-RUN.md](WALK-AND-RUN.md) | Keep one owner for each channel. No automatic vestibulo-ocular reflex or centre-of-mass/balance controller is documented |

Check signs with a one-pose render before applying a measured curve. Play the final body and face together at the
intended speed. Inspect target acquisition, stabilization, hand contact, blink reopening and conversational turn
changes. Check the eyes from the front and the head against the torso from the side. A clean contact report cannot
verify gaze or facial playback. These are authoring checks; this page does not claim a built gaze fixture.

## What is not known yet

- Human eye, head and trunk onset delays for ordinary **10–40°** shifts are not established as a reusable set here.
  The Barnes task and scene-viewing delays do not fill that gap.
- A curve for the head's share of a mid-size shift is missing. Fang's slopes do not have sufficiently defined axes
  in the retained record to reconstruct one.
- Conversation head-nod and tilt amplitudes, inter-blink interval distribution, and neck range were not established
  in this task research. This guide supplies no neck limit.
- Johansson's numerical gaze lead times were not verified from full text. The tea-making and Gregori measurements
  remain separate tasks, not substitutes for that missing result.
- The **800 ± 200 ms** head-yaw lead before a corner pivot was a search snippet only. It is excluded from targets.
- The claim that the head precedes gaze by **200–400 ms** (Sidenmark & Gellersen, 2019) was an unopened search
  snippet. It remains unresolved and is not a target. The duplicate turning listing's **407 ms / 50 ms** figures
  are also unverified; use only the separately cited Baker results above, with their event definition and uncertainty.
- Rhesus onset delays, head-share thresholds and peak eye speeds are animal evidence. They are not human presets.
- Blink closing speed had a suspect unit in Casse's abstract. No corrected speed is supplied here. Unnamed snippet
  estimates of blink duration and rate are excluded too.
- Dedicated eye/gaze/blink behaviour, automatic attention selection and a balance controller are not shown in the
  authoring guides. Verify the actual character's face controls and their playback before promising those results.
