# Human movement: lifting, carrying, pushing and pulling

[Human movement guides](HUMAN-MOVEMENT.md) · this page: how a person lifts, carries, pushes and pulls.

This guide says what a body does when it picks a load up off the floor, carries it, and pushes or pulls a cart. It
gives joint angles, the order and timing of events, how far the load sits from the body, and where the hands go. It
does not give a GraphCode recipe for a box, because the guides do not have one. It says which guide to use for each
piece.

**What the numbers are.** The sources are laboratory studies, mostly young healthy adults lifting 5-20 kg boxes.
Carrying rows name their own population. Many papers do not state their angle zero or segment definition. Where that
is so the row says "zero not stated". Each row ends with a source key in square brackets; the keys are listed under
"Sources". Labels:

- **C**: cited, full text read.
- **Ca**: cited, abstract only.
- **Cs**: cited through a named secondary page.
- **D**: derived here by arithmetic, which is shown.

Copy a whole consistent set from one row. Never mix values from different studies into one pose.

## What a correct-looking result is

- **A lift shows its technique.** A stoop (back bent, knees nearly straight), a semi-squat and a squat differ most at
  the knee. All three are real. People mostly use stoop or semi-squat and call it a squat.
- **The joints do not all move together.** Rising, the knee extends first, then the hip, then the lower back.
- **The load stays close to the body** at the start and through the lift.
- **A one-handed carry shows the load.** The head and chest tilt away from the loaded side and the free shoulder lifts
  out to the side.
- **A push leans into the cart. A pull leans back against it.** Hands are at a height that suits the job.

## The movement, in order

### A lift from the floor

Timings are for a young adult at a comfortable speed, unless the row says otherwise.

| Event | What was found | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Whole task from upright | **4-5 s**. Lowering and lifting phases each about **2 s** (15 men, 180 N load) | [BZ07] Ca/C |
| Bending down | Joints begin moving in order proximal to distal (lumbar, hip, knee, ankle). One subject, two-handed symmetric lift | [BL93] Ca, n = 1 |
| Coming up | The same joints move in the reverse order | [BL93] Ca, n = 1 |
| Early in the lift | Knee extension leads hip extension, which leads extension of the lumbar joints (39 subjects, light loads from 9 cm) | [BL95] Ca |
| Lift speed | Mean **0.59 m/s** (SD 0.14) squat and **0.60 m/s** (SD 0.10) stoop at comfortable speed (what point moved was not stated) | [HW09] C |
| The lower back's curve reverses | From kyphosis to lordosis about **50%** of the way through the squat lift and about **60%** through the stoop lift (26 young men) | [HW09] C |
| Peak load | Peak total and compressive loads came about **30% earlier** in the lifting cycle in squat than in stoop. Stoop peaked at **40%-70%** of the cycle | [VA21] C |

## Angles and distances

### The three lift techniques

Washmuth et al. 2022, a clinical review. Flexion angles; zero not stated in the fetch.

| Technique | Knee flexion | Trunk flexion | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stoop (bend the back) | under 45° | about 90° | [WA22] C |
| Semi-squat | about 90° | about 45° | [WA22] C |
| Squat (bend the knees) | about 135° | under 30° | [WA22] C |

What people actually do:

| Element | What was found | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Instructed "squat" in practice | 17 healthy young adults (8 men, 9 women) lifting a 15 kg box in a study of an active exoskeleton (four conditions). The squat lifts averaged about **56° trunk inclination**, which the authors say "would correspond more to a semi-squat" (the squat definition expects about 30°) | [HA26] C |
| Self-selected start posture | 39 subjects lifting light loads from 9 cm above the ground started in a posture intermediate between stoop and full squat | [BL95] Ca |
| Default technique | Stoop and semi-squat are common in athletes. Stoop is the more energy-sparing and quicker technique, "likely the reason many individuals use the stoop as their default" | [WA22] C |
| Start height changes the angles | Raising the start height from 9 cm to 63 cm accounted for 19%-67% of the variance in joint positions. The authors propose a postural index, knee flexion divided by the sum of ankle, hip and lumbar flexion, to classify postures independent of height | [BL97] Ca (the index itself: search snippet, not opened, [BL97S]) |
| Technique switches with height | 11 participants lifted and lowered with the lower shelf moved 1 cm per cycle between 10 and 50 cm. Squat pattern: moderate hip, knee and ankle range, ankle plantar flexion at the same time as knee and hip extension. Stoop pattern: knee range reduced, knee and hip extension accompanied by ankle dorsiflexion. Abrupt switches stoop to squat on the way down, squat to stoop on the way up. The average shelf height at the switch was 5 cm higher on the ascending trials | [BL01] Ca |
| Knee range is what differs | In 26 young men lifting 5, 10 and 15 kg boxes (34 x 34 x 27.5 cm) at comfortable speed, the knee had the largest range-of-motion difference between squat and stoop | [HW09] C |
| Lumbar range over the lift | 30 adults (20 men, 10 women, 31.8 ± 8.5 years) lifting a 15 kg box from the floor: lumbar lordosis range **24.2 ± 7.3°** freestyle, **25.1 ± 8.2°** squat, **35.9 ± 10.1°** stoop (angle zero not stated) | [VA21] C |
| Thorax and lumbar rotation | 15 men, 180 N load on a bar in front at 20 cm height, upright start: thorax rotations of 66.9° and 70° in stoop lifts against 38.4° and 49.7° in squat lifts (the fetched text elides which load case each value belongs to). Lumbar (T12-S1) rotation larger in stoop by 10.5° and 5.9° with and without the 180 N load | [BZ07] C |

### Load distance, foot placement and technique choice

| Element | What was found | Source |
|---|---|---|
| NIOSH horizontal distance H | Measured from the midpoint between the ankles to the hands (midpoint of the grip). Multiplier HM = 25/H: **1.00 at 25 cm or less; 0.40 at 63 cm; HM = 0 beyond 63 cm**. The CCOHS table stops at 60 cm (0.42); the other source states the range 25-63 cm. Arithmetic checks: 25/63 = 0.397, 25/60 = 0.417 | [N-UOEH], [N-CCOHS], [N-VULA] Cs; D |
| NIOSH vertical V and distance D | V is the hand height above the floor at the start of the lift: VM = 1 - 0.003 abs(V - 75), optimum **75 cm**, range 0-175 cm (the CCOHS table row at 70 cm shows 0.99, at 175 cm 0.70). D is the vertical travel of the load: DM = 0.82 + 4.5/D, range 25-175 cm | [N-UOEH], [N-CCOHS] Cs |
| NIOSH twist A | Asymmetry angle A of the body: AM = 1 - 0.0032 x A. Defined for 0°-135°, AM = 0 above 135°. The CCOHS table shows 90° at 0.71 and does not mention 135° (1 - 0.0032 x 90 = 0.712, D) | [N-UOEH], [N-VULA], [N-CCOHS] Cs; D |
| Load held further out in stoop | In the stoop lift the 180 N load was carried further forward of S1 than in the squat lift, mean difference under 88 mm | [BZ07] C |
| Load between the feet or knees | Review of stoop-versus-squat studies: net moments and compression were equal or somewhat higher in squat. Only when the load could be lifted from between the feet did squat give lower net moments (studies of marginal validity). Shear force and bending moment on the spine were lower in squat. No support for advising squat as such | [VD99] Ca |
| Feet behind or beside the box | 10 subjects, 10.5 kg boxes without handles. Lifting from 0.05 m with the feet behind the box, squat gave 19.9% (SD 8.7%) higher net moments and 17.0% (SD 13.2%) higher compression than stoop. With the feet beside the box the gaps fell to 12.8% and a non-significant 7.4%. From 0.5 m height the difference disappeared. Turning the knees outward in a squat gave values between squat and stoop. The authors explain the differences by the horizontal L5/S1 position relative to the load, upper-body acceleration and lumbar flexion | [KI04] Ca |
| Wide box that does not fit between the knees | 11 men: a "weight lifters' technique" (wide foot placement, moderate knee flexion, straight but not upright trunk) gave over 20% lower compression than free, squat and stoop lifts when the box had handles allowing a high grip, because the load stayed closer to L5/S1. At a box lifted by its bottom edge no technique was clearly better | [KI10] Ca |
| One leg beside the load | 12 men, 20 kg boxes. Narrow box from 290 mm: peak L5-S1 compression 5,060 N stoop, 3,980 N squat, 4,208 N straddle (one leg beside the box), 4,719 N one-leg kneeling. Wide box from 50 mm: 5,926 / 6,868 / 6,472 / 6,064 N in the same order. Conclusion: no single technique suits all conditions | [KI06] Ca |
| Hand on the thigh | Supporting the upper body with a hand on the thigh cut average peak total moments by 17%-25% depending on technique, up to 28% in all techniques except stoop | [KI16] Ca |
| Which technique to pick | 30 students lifting with three techniques: authors recommend semi-squat for daily lifting, squat for heavy loads, stoop only for light things | [WZ12] Ca |

**Where the sources disagree.** [WZ12] recommends squat for heavy loads. [VD99] found no support for advising squat as
such. Show the technique the character's situation calls for; do not present squat as the "correct" lift.

### Carrying

| Element | What was found | Source |
|---|---|---|
| One hand, walking (older adults) | 31 older adults (15 fallers, 16 non-fallers) carried a dumbbell worth 2%-10% of body mass in one hand. Load-dependent adjustments: tilt of head and thorax toward the side opposite the load and more abduction of the opposite shoulder. At higher loads more activity in the opposite gluteus medius. Step timing and length were not affected. No angles were in the abstract | [SE26] Ca |
| One hand, walking (women) | Five young and six older women with a hand-held bag: trunk, head and upper arm were recruited. Opposite hip abduction torque rose, load-side hip torque fell. The only group difference was shoulder abduction on the opposite side, larger in the older women even without a bag | [MA08] Ca |
| One hand, walking (adults) | 19 adults carried a dumbbell of 10% or 20% of body weight in the right or left hand. The trunk bent toward the loaded or the unloaded side depending on hand and load (the abstract's wording). The amplitude grew with load, stride width fell, and the legs were progressively inclined toward the unloaded side | [ZH10] Ca |
| One hand versus both | Six participants carrying buckets: 30 kg in one hand gave low-back compression above 2,800 N. Splitting it between both hands gave 1,570 N (44% lower). 30 kg in each hand gave lower compression than 30 kg in one hand | [MG13] Ca |
| Two people, one hand each | 25 men carried 3.5, 7 and 10.5 kg alone and 7, 14 and 21 kg in teams, one-handed over 30 m (IMU angles). Trunk extension rose significantly with weight in team carrying. Taller team members differed in head and trunk lateral bending | [TEAM] search snippet, not opened |
| Backpack, children, trunk flexion | 12 boys (mean 12.3 years, 151.3 cm, 46.6 kg), walking: trunk flexion **13.4° (SD 2.8)** at 5% body weight, **15.5° (SD 3.6)** at 10%, **18.3° (SD 3.3)** at 15% (angle zero not stated). Pack position (T7 15.5°, T12 16.6°, L3 15.1°) made no significant difference | [CM18] C |
| Backpack, soldiers, walking | 20 male soldiers, loads of 8, 16, 40 and 50 kg, self-paced: the trunk flexed significantly further forward with load and the head moved forward (craniovertebral angle fell), "in concert with the trunk". Knee range 21.1 ± 3.0° rose to 25.5 ± 2.3° and femur range 33.9 ± 7.1° to 37.8 ± 1.5° from the 8 kg to the 50 kg condition | [AT06] Ca |
| Backpack, standing | 20 male cadets standing for 60 s with 0, 16.0 kg (20% body weight) and 20.5 kg (26%): forward trunk lean and pelvic rotation were not significantly changed. The same paper cites soldiers (mean 74.9 kg) who leaned forward significantly walking with 39.95 and 50.05 kg compared with loads under 15.96 kg | [ST17] C (the cited soldier study: Cs) |
| Backpack, gait timing | Children 10%/15%/20% of body weight: velocity, cadence and double-support time differed significantly at 20%, more when the load sat low on the back. Forward trunk lean was greater walking than standing [SI09]. Adolescent girls 0%-15%: speed and cadence fell and double support rose, with a critical load near 10% of body weight [CH05]. Two boys: trunk lean did not rise in step with the load [GO02] | [SI09], [CH05], [GO02] Ca |
| Load in front of the chest | 30 adults, 0%, 10%, 20% of body weight carried in front, treadmill 1.2 m/s: trunk angle increased significantly, which the authors describe as backward lean, at touchdown and at lift-off. Step length did not differ [SI22]. 47 adults aged 20-80 with a 4.5 kg chest load: lumbar lordosis increased during parts of the gait cycle and trunk inclination was reduced through the whole cycle [BA26] | [SI22], [BA26] Ca |

**Where the sources disagree.** For a one-handed carry, [SE26] reports head and thorax tilting toward the side opposite
the load. [ZH10]'s abstract says the trunk bent toward the loaded or the unloaded side depending on hand and load. The
authors tie the difference to load, task experience and handedness. Pick one source's whole set for one pose.

### Pushing and pulling

Almost all sources are abstracts or a review. They give where the hands go and which way the body leans, more than
lean angles.

| Element | What was found | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Hand height, overview | A review of cart pushing and pulling: ideal handle positions range from hip to shoulder height. Handles should allow different hand positions between hip or waist and shoulder height. The review's Table 3 lists handle heights in studies as "knuckle, elbow, shoulder", "Sh & Hh" (abbreviation not expanded in the fetch) and "mid-thigh, elbow, 0.1 m above head" | [AW17] C |
| Hand height, lower back | Measured spinal compression: handles at waist level for pulling and shoulder level for pushing (as cited in the review, its ref. 11) | [AW17] Cs |
| Hand height, shoulder | Net shoulder moments were lower when pushing and pulling at shoulder height (the review's ref. 13) | [AW17] Cs |
| Hand height, laboratory | 6 subjects of 50-80 kg, forces 98, 196 and 294 N, three heights **660, 1090 and 1520 mm**, speeds 1.8 and 3.6 km/h. A 1090 mm handle was better for pushing and a 1520 mm handle better for pulling for low-back load. Pushing loaded the low back less than pulling. Body weight mattered more in pulling (50% more load from 50 to 80 kg) than in pushing (25%) | [LE91] Ca |
| Hand height, another lab | One or two hands, three cart weights, two handle heights: recommends low cart weights and pushing or pulling at shoulder height | [HO04] Ca |
| Lean direction | When pushing, experienced workers put the upper body in front of the base of support, leaning into the load. When pulling, behind the base of support, leaning back against it (the review, citing its ref. 11) | [AW17] Cs |
| Technique matters more than load | 9 men (4 firefighters trained in pushing and pulling, 5 students) pushed and pulled at two handle heights with three loads. Firefighters produced less spinal compression and shear and less muscle activation than students. The authors say technique can matter more than the physical variables | [LM06] Ca |
| Push versus pull posture | Delivery trolleys: pushing produced more trunk flexion. Pulling gave more upper-limb muscle activation and more flexion of the lower arms and neck. Pulling a hand cart loaded the low back most. Transport speeds **0.66-1.15 m/s**, pushing and straight lines faster than pulling and curves (trunk flexion estimated from video with OpenPose) | [DE26] Ca |
| Foot placement | 13 young men and 10 young women, maximal push and pull exertions at hand heights of 48, 84, 120 and 156 cm. Direction and height changed the foot placement. Pushing needed more front-back space than pulling. The trailing foot position, given as female as a percentage of male, ranged 77%-90% (pushing) and 80%-93% (pulling) across the heights. Advice: allow front-back space of about **70% of body stature** | [LT18] Ca |
| Slippery floor | 8 men pushed and pulled a four-wheeled trolley over 3 m on a good-grip and a reduced-grip floor. The maximum acceptable initial force did not change, but knee flexion and trunk extension changed significantly. Handlers adjusted posture and the direction of hand and foot forces to compensate | [BO06] Ca |
| Hand orientation | Off-axis hand forces diminish with the forearm neutral, palms facing up (the review's ref. 27) | [AW17] Cs |
| Walking pace | One cited study in the review used a gait pace of 80 steps/min (its Table 3, Kao 2015) | [AW17] Cs |

**Where the sources disagree.** The best height to pull at is waist level ([AW17] ref. 11), 1520 mm ([LE91]) or
shoulder height ([HO04]). All three are shown. Do not average them.

## The most common mistakes

All are *derived* from the rows above.

1. **A squat lift with the trunk near vertical.** The squat definition allows under 30° of trunk flexion, but
   instructed squats were measured near 56°. A genuinely upright trunk with deep knees is not what people do.
2. **Knee, hip and lumbar all extending at the same instant.** The sources give a lead order: knee, then hip, then
   the lower back.
3. **A load held more than 63 cm from the ankle midpoint.** That is outside the NIOSH range. Also, a stoop and a squat
   that look the same from the knee: the knee range is the main difference between them.
4. **A heavy one-handed carry with no head, thorax or free-shoulder response**, or **a front-held load with the trunk
   leaning forward** (the front-carry study found a backward-lean trunk angle).
5. **A push and a pull with the same lean.** Lean in when pushing; lean back when pulling. Also, both feet side by side
   during a maximal effort: the advice is about 70% of stature of front-back space.
6. **The same posture on a slippery floor as on a grippy one.** Knee flexion and trunk extension changed
   significantly. Also, hands at one fixed height for every task, and a trolley moved faster than the 0.66-1.15 m/s
   listed for delivery trolleys.

## How to author it in GraphCode

The guides have no lift, carry, push or pull recipe. Nothing in them has been built for a box or a cart. What they do
have is the parts. Use them with the numbers above.

| You want | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Legs bending as the hips drop, feet staying put | `plant(leg_R, on=$Floor, contact=Sole_R, weight=...)` and `swing` on thigh, calf and foot, per [ANIMATION.md](ANIMATION.md#compose-movement-in-stages) | The guide's rule: prefer `plant()` on the floor with the rig's sole contacts, so the IK bends the knees as the hips drop |
| A deep squat that needs more ankle than the rig has | `shift(Root, ...)` to move the hips back, and lean the trunk forward over them | ANIMATION.md: do not push the ankle past its range. A squat needs about 135° of knee flexion; check the rig's `calf` range allows it (RifleMan's allows (-5, 150), [STAIRS.md](STAIRS.md)) |
| The knee, hip and back leading in order | `swing` on `thigh` fold, `calf` fold and `spine_01..03` fold, each with its own `profile`, started in the order above | Joint mapping: [WALK-AND-RUN.md](WALK-AND-RUN.md#2-draw-the-legs-in-place). Check every sign with a one-pose test |
| A trunk lean | `swing(spine_0N, axis=fold, by=...)`. On a rig built like RifleMan a positive fold leans forward | Backpack lean: 13.4°-18.3° of flexion in the children's study (zero not stated) |
| A hand on the thigh while lifting | `reach(arm_R, to=$Point)` to a point on the thigh bone, `point(bone=..., at=(...))` ([POSES.md](POSES.md#hold-an-object-while-moving)) | Reaching a body-attached point is supported by the guides. Not built for this |
| Two hands on a box | The two-hand method of [HANDS.md Step 9](HANDS.md#step-9-a-rifle-in-both-hands-then-walk-with-it): `reach(arm_R, ...)`, `reach(arm_L, ...)`, `collision(self, $Box)` | The nearest built example is a rifle. A box is not built |
| A walk while carrying | [WALK-AND-RUN.md](WALK-AND-RUN.md): lay the held pose over the walk with `clip(from=$Walk) { $Gripped }` | A heavy load's effect on the gait (a shorter step, more double support) is not authored for you. The sources show it only for backpacks |
| A cart moving | Character movement owns the travel, with `[root_motion]` ([ANIMATION.md](ANIMATION.md#what-each-implemented-instruction-does)) | One owner of travel. Do not let the animation and the game both move the root |

Two cautions from the guides:

- **A push or a pull applies no force.** There is no force or load verb. You author the lean and the arm posture, and
  you judge by eye whether the body looks loaded.
- **Balance is static.** `balance()` keeps the body's mass over the planted soles ([MOVE-BALANCE.md](MOVE-BALANCE.md));
  it does not model a pull's force or momentum. A body that leans back to pull a cart is authored by hand, with
  `balance(..., at=)` to say where its weight should sit.
- **`[share=]` cannot make a push lean, and it undoes a lean you authored** (measured 2026-10-23, BlueEdge's guard and
  shove). A share turns the chest's FRONT toward the hand's place; a push's hands are at chest height, so the turn
  levels the chest. A shove key built on a body already folded 25 degrees kept only 17.8 of it with `[share=0.4]` on the
  reach, and its neck base ended 5 cm BEHIND the toe tips; with the share off, the authored fold was kept exactly and the
  neck base sat 1.42 cm in front of them. Balance was not the cause either way (1.17 cm of a 35 cm anchor budget). Author
  the lean, leave the share off that reach, and let the arm reach from where the lean leaves the shoulder. The share also
  reported itself 8.4-10.3 degrees short of its own ask while it was fighting the fold.

## What is not known yet

From the research note's own gaps:

- **Carrying:** no numeric lateral trunk-lean angle for a hand-carried load was found (the one paper that might have
  held it returned only a verification page). No front-carry arm or elbow angle, no shoulder-carry data and no carry
  stride-length numbers.
- **NIOSH 94-110 itself could not be read.** Its PDF is a scanned image or over the size limit. Every NIOSH value above
  comes from reproductions ([N-UOEH], [N-CCOHS], [N-VULA]).
- **Lift-off angles** for squat and stoop lifts were not recovered, except the Washmuth definitions.
- **Pushing and pulling:** no source gave trunk inclination, body-to-handle distance or foot-to-foot distance in cm or
  degrees. The HSE guidance PDF (INDG478) returned only encoded data, the Liberty Mutual tables returned a 403, and
  the searches for numeric lean angles returned nothing usable. Hand-force magnitudes were left out because they do not
  change the pose.
- **A door's push or pull posture** is not sourced either. See [HUMAN-DOORS-AND-DEVICES.md](HUMAN-DOORS-AND-DEVICES.md).
- **Not in GraphCode:** no box, cart or load recipe is built, no force or balance verb exists, and the effect of a load
  on gait is not authored for you.
