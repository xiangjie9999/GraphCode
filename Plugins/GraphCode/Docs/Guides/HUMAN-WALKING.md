# Human movement: walking, what a normal walk looks like

[Human movement guides](HUMAN-MOVEMENT.md) · this page: what a normal walk looks like, in numbers.

This guide states what a healthy adult's walk at a self-selected speed does: how fast, how long a stride, how long each
foot is down, what each joint does through the cycle, and how the pelvis, trunk, arms and head move. Use it to decide
what your walk should look like and to check a clip against a person. **How to build the clip** (feet that roll and do
not slide, a body the feet carry) is in [WALK-AND-RUN.md](WALK-AND-RUN.md); this guide does not repeat it.
[HUMAN-MOVEMENT.md](HUMAN-MOVEMENT.md) explains the angle conventions and evidence words used below.

## What a correct-looking walk is

All numbers are in the tables further down, each with its source.

- **Speed and rhythm.** An adult at a preferred pace walks about 1.3 to 1.4 m/s (1.36 ± 0.17 in 17 young men), at
  about 110 steps a minute (110.4 ± 8.4), with a stride of about 1.47 m (1.47 ± 0.13).
- **Support timing.** One foot is on the ground about 60–65 % of its cycle. Both feet are down twice (about 16 % each
  in one measured take), and the other foot strikes at 50 %.
- **The leg.** The heel strikes first with the knee nearly straight and the hip flexed about 25° (clinical angles).
  The knee bends to about 15° as weight comes on, to about 40° as the other foot lands, and reaches about 60° early
  in swing (72.2° in one laboratory take). It is about 0° again at the next contact.
- **The foot rolls.** The heel rises at the start of terminal stance (31 % of the cycle). The toes bend about 65°
  (clinical) as the body pushes off, and the foot leaves the floor at about 62 %.
- **The body.** The pelvis turns 14.1° (range), rises and falls about 4.9 cm and sways about 5.9 cm side to side (one
  take). The shoulders turn against the pelvis. Each arm swings forward with the opposite leg. In the one full-body
  take the elbow never straightens (30–49°), but another study says walking elbows are nearly straight (see the arm
  rows below). The head stays near level.

## The six most common mistakes

1. **Making a walk faster by raising the play rate.** A faster walk has longer **and** quicker steps, less double
   support, more pelvis turn and more vertical bounce, but less side sway. Speeding a clip up changes only cadence.
2. **Mixing two angle conventions in one character.** Clinical (Rancho Los Amigos) angles measure the thigh against
   vertical. Laboratory model angles measure it against the pelvis. Hip at contact is 25° in one and 27.7° in the
   other; hip extension in terminal stance is 20° and −16.3°; the swing knee peaks at 60° and 72.2°. Take one source
   for one character.
3. **A frozen pelvis and trunk.** The pelvis turns 14.1° and the shoulder girdle turns the opposite way (12° pelvis, 7°
   shoulder girdle in one study). A rig that locks hip and trunk twist cannot do this; see "Ranges a walk needs" below.
4. **Lifting the heel at the last moment.** A real heel starts to rise at the start of terminal stance, well before
   toe-off. A curve that holds the foot flat and then turns it in two frames looks like a snap
   ([WALK-AND-RUN.md](WALK-AND-RUN.md)).
5. **Support that hands over with no overlap.** At a normal walk each foot is down about 60–65 % of its cycle, so
   there are two double-support periods (about 16 % each in the one measured take). Weights that switch at exactly
   50 % give a walk with no double support (derived).
6. **Using the wrong "step width".** Heel to heel, the step width is 8.1 cm (men) and 7.1 cm (women). The 0.17 m
   "stride width" in one reference is probably measured between markers, not heel centres; do not use it as the gap
   between the feet.

Rules of thumb from these rows (derived):

- At a normal adult walk a foot is on the ground about 60–65 % of its cycle, both feet are down twice (about 16 %
  each in the one measured take: 0–15.6 % and 49.7–66.2 %), and the other foot strikes at 50 %.
- **Speed (m/s) = stride length (m) × cadence (steps/min) ÷ 120.** Check: 1.47 × 110.4 ÷ 120 = 1.352 (the paper's
  speed is 1.36); Murray's men, 1.58 × 117 ÷ 120 = 1.54 (the paper's 1.54). Two of Pietraszewski's step lengths add to
  0.61 + 0.64 = 1.25 m, not the 1.47 m stride, so build from stride and cadence, which reproduce the speed.
- For stride length on a character of another size, scale by hip height, not by a body-height ratio
  ([HUMAN-BODY-SIZE-AND-BALANCE.md](HUMAN-BODY-SIZE-AND-BALANCE.md)).
- Pietraszewski's "stride width" of 0.17 ± 0.03 m is far wider than Murray's and Hollman's step widths. It is
  probably a different definition (between markers, not heel centres). Use Murray or Hollman for a heel-to-heel
  step width.
- Öberg's abstract (abstract): significant sex differences exist in all gait parameters; age changed speed and step
  length but not step frequency. The step length for men 20–29 is printed "m" in the quote and must mean cm.

## 4. Perry's eight phases and the clinical angles

Phase boundaries, % of the cycle from initial contact of the same foot (AAPM&R, secondary). What happens is the
clinical Rancho/Perry description (Godges (Kaiser) and AAPM&R, secondary). The angles are against vertical.

| Phase | % cycle | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Initial contact | 0–2 | heel strikes; hip flexed about 25°, knee extended, ankle neutral |
| Loading response | 2–12 | knee flexes to about 15°, ankle plantarflexes about 10° to lower the forefoot; other foot leaves at the end |
| Mid stance | 12–31 | body passes over the foot; ankle dorsiflexes (about 5° in AAPM&R, maximum about 10° late in Godges) |
| Terminal stance | 31–50 | **heel rises at its start**; hip reaches its maximum extension (20°), knee near full extension, ankle 10–20° dorsiflexion |
| Pre-swing | 50–62 | other foot strikes at 50 %; knee flexes to about 40°, ankle to about 20° plantarflexion, first MTP extended about 65°; toe-off at about 62 % |
| Initial swing | 62–75 | knee reaches its maximum flexion, about 60° (Godges, OUHSC) |
| Mid swing | 75–87 | hip reaches its maximum flexion, about 25° |
| Terminal swing | 87–100 | knee extends to about 0° ready for contact |

Where the clinical and laboratory sources disagree:

| Event | Clinical (Rancho) | Laboratory (one OpenSim take, measured) |
|---|---|---|
| Hip at contact | 25° | 27.7° |
| Hip extension in terminal stance | 20° (against vertical) | −16.3° (against a pelvis that is itself tilted) |

| Plantarflexion | 20° in pre-swing | −16.4° just after toe-off |

The clinical 65° of toe extension at pre-swing and the measured 29.8° relative to standing also differ: the measured
value subtracts the standing angle, and the cohort differs. Neither is a whole-cycle toe curve.

## Putting the numbers into a clip

GraphCode does not invent a gait: you draw the legs, and the solver keeps the feet honest and moves the body
([WALK-AND-RUN.md](WALK-AND-RUN.md)). There is no speed to type; the body's travel comes from the planted feet.

| A number in this guide | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| Heel strike at 0 %, toe-off at about 62 % | the plant's `weight=` profile. WALK-AND-RUN uses `OnGroundR = profile(0=1, 0.6=1, 0.62=0, 0.98=0, loop=true)`; the left foot is the same half a stride later |
| The other foot strikes at 50 % | the left leg is the right one half a stride later, `[phase=0.5]` |
| Hip, knee, ankle and toe curves | `swing(..., axis=fold, by=$Profile)` with a looping profile, drawn in place; the table in [HUMAN-MOVEMENT.md](HUMAN-MOVEMENT.md) gives the control for each anatomical angle |
| Pelvis rise (4.9 cm) | `shift(Root, axis=<up>, by=$Bob)`, in centimetres, along the Root's own up axis |
| Hip turn, trunk counter-turn, step width | rig ranges first (`twists=` on the thighs and spine joints), then `swing(..., axis=side, by=...)` for a step width; see "Ranges a walk needs" |
| A different body size | scale the clip's timing and stride by the rules in [HUMAN-BODY-SIZE-AND-BALANCE.md](HUMAN-BODY-SIZE-AND-BALANCE.md) |

**Ranges a walk needs.** On the example character a walk needs −17° of ankle plantarflexion, −13° to +6° of hip turn
and ±13° of trunk counter-turn, against ankle −10° and a locked hip and trunk as built ([WALK-AND-RUN.md](WALK-AND-RUN.md),
measured 2026-10-07). A range that is too short does not fail the build: it prints `WARNING joint limit` and the foot
stops short.

**What GraphCode does not do here.** Pelvis tilt, list and turn are not mapped in the walk and run example rig (its
pelvis joint is rigid; the MetaHuman template, [Examples/MetaHumanBody.gcrig](Examples/MetaHumanBody.gcrig), declares
`pelvis` fold, side and twist), and the full-body run is a first pass ([WALK-AND-RUN.md](WALK-AND-RUN.md), "What it
does not do yet"). Measured curves rarely end where they start, so a looping walk needs a loop-fitting choice that is
an authoring change, not a measurement; say so in a comment.

  2019: 50 adults, 5 speeds): neither paper tabulates the values, which exist only in the raw data.
- A reading of Pietraszewski's peak angles by eye from a figure (about ±2–3°) is **not** used here. A search summary
  claiming walking shoulder flexion–extension of "20°-25°" and elbow "15-30°" could not be traced and is unused.
- Any whole-cycle toe curve (swing is not measured).
